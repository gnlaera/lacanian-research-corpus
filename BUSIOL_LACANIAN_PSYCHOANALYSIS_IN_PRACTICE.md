# 31_BUSIOL_LACANIAN_PSYCHOANALYSIS_IN_PRACTICE

## Source identity and status

**Diego Busiol (ed.), _Lacanian Psychoanalysis in Practice: Insights from Fourteen Psychoanalysts_**  
Routledge, 2022.  
ISBN 978-1-138-36246-8 (hardback); 978-1-138-36247-5 (paperback); 978-0-429-43206-4 (ebook).  
DOI: 10.4324/9780429432064.

**Corpus status:** secondary and tertiary Lacanian clinical source. This is not a primary text by Jacques Lacan and should not be used as direct evidence for what Lacan himself said. It is a multi-author clinical volume in which practicing analysts from several Italian and French Lacanian traditions explain how they listen, formulate cases, and intervene. The contributors share Freud and Lacan as major reference points but do not form a theoretically uniform school.

**Purpose of this file:** a structured analytical capture for use alongside the primary Lacan corpus, especially for clinical understanding, self-analysis, and thinking about couple dynamics. It is a synthesis, not a reproduction of the copyrighted book. Where a concept is an author's own development, or belongs to a particular post-Lacanian school, that provenance is made explicit.

# 1. Why this book is highly relevant to the Lacanian corpus

This book fills a gap between Lacan's conceptual teaching and the practical question that repeatedly arises when one tries to use Lacanian theory clinically: **what does an analyst actually listen for, and what can the analyst do with what is heard?**

The strongest parts of the book do not try to translate Lacan into a list of concepts. They show a clinical stance. Across contributors, the recurrent emphasis is on listening to the exact words a subject uses, allowing equivocation and contradiction to remain productive, distinguishing demand from desire, identifying forms of repetitive enjoyment, and avoiding the temptation to impose a ready-made psychological explanation.

The volume is also useful because it tries to update Lacanian clinical thinking for forms of suffering that do not necessarily present like the classical Freudian neuroses. It discusses panic, eating disorders, depression, addiction, so-called borderline or limit states, narcissistic fragility, and what Massimo Recalcati calls the **clinic of the void**. These chapters are not interchangeable with Lacan's own structural clinic, but they show how later Lacanian clinicians have tried to adapt analytic work to patients who may arrive with little curiosity about the unconscious and a strong demand for immediate relief.

For self-analysis, the most useful material is concentrated in Chapters 2 and 3. Busiol repeatedly asks the reader to notice exact signifiers, slips, apparently accidental word choices, recurring metaphors, contradictions, negations, and the difference between what one consciously wants and what one's speech suggests one desires. This can be turned into a disciplined method of reflection without pretending that self-analysis is equivalent to an actual analysis.

For couple therapy, the book is useful in a different way from Mary Morgan's _Couple Relations_. Morgan focuses on the relation as a system. Busiol and the other Lacanian contributors focus more on the structure of each subject's speech, desire, fantasy, enjoyment, and relation to the Other. Used together, the two books allow two complementary questions:

- What is the couple producing together?
- What position is each subject taking in what they say and repeat?

The volume also contains several points of direct relevance to couples: demand for recognition, jealousy, hysterical dissatisfaction, obsessional control, the wish to know what the other wants, problems of sexuality and sexual identity, the relation with the maternal Other, the use of the partner as a support for narcissistic consistency, and repetitive forms of enjoyment that can persist even when they damage the relationship.

### Bottom-line classification

- **Very high value:** Lacanian listening, free association, free-floating attention, signifiers, misunderstanding, interpretation, desire, jouissance, fantasy, symptom, transference, clinical stance.
- **High value:** contemporary symptom formations, panic, depression, eating disorders, addiction, maternal relation, jealousy.
- **Medium-high value:** case formulation for couple dynamics when used alongside a specifically relational source such as Morgan.
- **Medium value:** historical material on Lacan in Italy and the institutional development of Italian Lacanian psychoanalysis.
- **Main risk:** treating the contributors' contemporary formulations as if they were Lacan's own doctrine.

# 2. Source boundaries: there is no single "Lacanian psychoanalysis" in this volume

Busiol explicitly presents the Italian Lacanian field as heterogeneous. Contributors come from different associations and intellectual lineages, including traditions connected to Jacques-Alain Miller, Charles Melman, and other independent developments. The editor's point is that clinical practice cannot simply be derived from a doctrine and then applied mechanically.

This is important for corpus management. The book should be read on at least four levels.

## 2.1 Primary Lacanian concepts

Some concepts are clearly taken from Lacan and can be cross-checked against primary sources:

- signifier;
- the big Other and small other;
- desire and demand;
- object _a_;
- jouissance;
- fantasy;
- gaze and voice;
- castration;
- Name-of-the-Father;
- subject supposed to know;
- the four discourses;
- the Real, Symbolic, and Imaginary;
- transference;
- interpretation by equivocation;
- identification with the symptom or sinthome.

## 2.2 Post-Lacanian clinical developments

Other concepts are later clinical elaborations that use Lacan as a reference but should not be attributed directly to him:

- Recalcati's **clinic of the void** and contrast with the clinic of lack;
- the contemporary **new symptoms** framework;
- formulations of panic as a modern phenomenon;
- Cosenza's **clinic of excess** in eating disorders;
- contemporary analyses of narcissistic fragility and limit states;
- Busiol's contrast between the classical patient who brings an interpretable symptom and the contemporary patient who may bring an action, body symptom, or identity.

## 2.3 School-specific extensions

Some chapters contain concepts from Armando Verdiglione and the cyphermatic tradition. Giancarlo Calciolari and Gabriele Lodari use terms such as the tripartition of the sign, logic of the word, maternal fantasy, name-function, mirror-gaze-voice in specific configurations, and the narrative as an originary pragmatic structure. These may be clinically interesting, but they are not standard Lacanian doctrine.

**Notebook rule:** when citing these ideas, attribute them to the individual author or to Verdiglione's tradition rather than writing "Lacan says".

## 2.4 Individual clinical judgments

The book contains judgments about contemporary society, sexuality, gender, family structure, psychotherapy, diagnostic classification, and the effects of capitalism. These often express a contributor's analytic orientation. They should be stored as **attributed clinical interpretations**, not as settled empirical findings.

This provenance distinction is essential if the notebook is to remain rigorous.

# 3. The book's clinical architecture

The book can be reduced to six recurring propositions.

## 3.1 Listen to speech, not to a psychological type

The object of psychoanalytic listening is not primarily the patient's personality, behavior, diagnosis, or intended message. It is the speech that is actually produced.

This means paying attention to:

- exact word choice;
- repeated words and metaphors;
- slips and misstatements;
- negations;
- contradictions;
- unusual phrases;
- jokes;
- equivocations;
- places where the speaker hesitates or changes direction;
- words borrowed from family members or authority figures;
- sudden shifts in pronouns, tense, or position.

The central practical rule is: **do not silently replace the patient's word with a synonym.** In ordinary conversation that may be harmless. In analysis it can erase precisely the signifier through which something unconscious is being expressed.

## 3.2 Suspend immediate understanding

The book repeatedly distinguishes analytic listening from ordinary communication. Ordinary communication aims to grasp intended meaning quickly. Analytic listening delays closure.

Freud's free-floating or evenly suspended attention is interpreted as a willingness not to decide too quickly what a statement means. A word may only acquire analytic significance through later associations. What seems irrelevant at first can retroactively reorganize what came before.

This is closely related to Lacan's emphasis on **après-coup**, or retroaction.

## 3.3 Treat misunderstanding as productive

In most forms of communication, misunderstanding is something to eliminate. In this book, misunderstanding can reveal the gap between intended meaning and the effects of the signifier.

This does not mean deliberately misunderstanding the patient. It means not assuming that conscious intention exhausts what was said.

A useful formula for self-analysis is:

> What did I say that I did not intend to say, and what does that unintended wording allow me to hear?

## 3.4 Desire is not identical with conscious wanting

The book's most important distinction is between what a person says they want and what appears to organize their desire.

Conscious wanting can be expressed as a goal: a partner, a job, certainty, happiness, relief from a symptom. Desire is inferred from the pattern of speech, repetition, fantasy, avoidance, attraction, inhibition, and satisfaction.

Desire is therefore not a hidden preference waiting to be discovered like a fact. It is a structure that appears through its effects.

## 3.5 Symptoms also contain satisfaction

A symptom is not treated only as a malfunction. Freud's substitute satisfaction and Lacan's jouissance are central. A symptom may hurt, constrain, humiliate, or frustrate the subject while simultaneously supplying a form of satisfaction that is difficult to relinquish.

This is clinically decisive. The question is not only:

> Why does this happen to me?

but also:

> What form of satisfaction is repeated here, even though I consciously dislike the result?

## 3.6 Contemporary patients may arrive without a question

Several contributors argue that many contemporary patients present differently from the classical neurotic model. Instead of bringing a symptom experienced as enigmatic, they may bring:

- panic;
- a repetitive act;
- self-harm;
- compulsive consumption;
- eating restriction or bingeing;
- a substance;
- depression or emptiness;
- a body-image problem;
- an identity fixed to a diagnostic label;
- a demand for immediate regulation.

The preliminary task may therefore be to help convert a demand for symptom removal into a question in which the subject can become implicated.

# 4. Introduction: clinical listening as the book's leitmotif

Busiol frames the whole volume around the experience of practicing psychoanalysis in Hong Kong with patients from many cultural backgrounds. He describes a clinical context characterized by speed, mobility, commercial pragmatism, international migration, and weak continuity of social and symbolic reference points.

He observes that some patients arrive feeling uprooted, without a stable sense of home or direction. They may move frequently, live between countries, and enjoy forms of freedom that are simultaneously liberating and disorienting. The clinical issue is not that mobility itself is pathological. The question is what happens when freedom from inherited structures is not accompanied by the construction of new symbolic coordinates.

The introduction establishes three purposes for the volume:

1. rethink clinical work with contemporary patients;
2. offer psychoanalytic listening as a resource to clinicians from other orientations;
3. show an international audience what a contemporary Lacanian clinic can look like in practice.

Busiol's key argument is that psychoanalysis cannot be manualized without losing something essential. The analyst cannot know in advance which intervention will work because the intervention must arise from the analysand's speech. The direction of the cure is therefore tied more closely to ethics, desire, and contingency than to a standardized protocol.

The editor also warns against the fantasy of a single Lacanian method. Each contributor has a different analytic formation and style. The book is therefore best used as a **portfolio of clinical positions**, not a manual.

### Practical implication

When using the book for self-analysis or couple reflection, do not ask, "What would a Lacanian do?" as though there were one procedural answer. Ask instead:

- What speech is being produced?
- What signifiers organize it?
- What desire is implied?
- What satisfaction is repeated?
- What kind of intervention would open speech rather than close it?

# 5. Chapter 1: The evolution of psychic symptoms from Freud's hysteria to today

## 5.1 The unconscious is not just a body of knowledge

Busiol begins by distinguishing knowledge **about** the unconscious from knowledge that **comes from** the unconscious. One can read psychoanalytic theory, but the unconscious becomes clinically relevant only in singular formations such as dreams, slips, forgetfulness, jokes, symptoms, and unexpected associations.

This is why psychoanalysis is not reducible to an academic theory. Its knowledge is generated case by case.

## 5.2 The contemporary demand

Busiol contrasts the classical psychoanalytic patient with many contemporary patients who may not arrive with a clear demand for analysis. Some want to "try" therapy, seek advice, want rapid symptom relief, or remain uncertain about whether they are committed to the process.

He associates this with a wider shift in social discourse. Contemporary culture often emphasizes autonomy, efficiency, self-realization, performance, unlimited possibility, rapid satisfaction, and continuous consumption.

His clinical thesis is that symptoms are partly shaped by the discourse in which subjects live.

## 5.3 From prohibition to the difficulty of limits

Busiol contrasts two broad configurations:

- the classical neurotic problem of desire constrained by prohibition;
- contemporary problems in which subjects struggle because there is too little symbolic limitation.

In his account, slogans of limitless possibility can coexist with panic, lack of direction, addictions, eating disorders, depression, impulsivity, and difficulty tolerating frustration.

The point is not that freedom causes pathology. The more precise claim is that desire requires some structure of lack and limitation. If everything is imagined to be immediately available, the subject can have difficulty locating what is actually desired.

## 5.4 Hysteria has not necessarily disappeared

The chapter asks whether diagnostic categories disappear because structures disappear or because symptoms take different forms.

Busiol surveys authors who argue that hysteria may now be diagnosed under other labels, including some somatic, borderline, or eating-disorder presentations. He does not claim that all such cases are hysteria. The methodological point is that descriptive diagnosis should not automatically be equated with psychoanalytic structure.

## 5.5 Classical and so-called new symptoms

The chapter summarizes a post-Lacanian distinction often made in contemporary clinical writing.

Classical neurotic symptoms tend to be treated as meaningful formations of the unconscious. They often invite interpretation and can be linked to the subject's history.

So-called new symptoms may be more directly enacted in the body or through behavior, may provide identity, and may initially appear to the subject as meaningless or simply as something to eliminate.

Busiol draws on Paul Verhaeghe and others to describe three tendencies:

- greater involvement of the body in real acts such as cutting, substance use, eating restriction, bingeing, or somatization;
- greater reliance on enactment rather than fantasy;
- weaker apparent linkage between the symptom and a personal narrative.

These are tendencies, not diagnostic laws.

## 5.6 Psychoanalysis is not symptom optimization

Busiol rejects the idea that analytic treatment is simply a service that removes a problem and returns the person to normal functioning. A symptom may be the entry point into a wider inquiry into fantasy, desire, identifications, and repetition.

The question becomes:

> Can the symptom become a starting point for knowledge rather than merely an obstacle to be removed?

## 5.7 Relevance for self-analysis

When you notice a symptom, repeated conflict, or pattern, separate four questions:

1. What do I consciously want to stop?
2. What does this symptom make possible for me?
3. What identity does the symptom support?
4. What question about my life would emerge if the symptom were not immediately treated as a defect?

# 6. Chapter 2: How to listen in analysis

This is the most practically useful chapter in the book for a self-analysis notebook.

## 6.1 The object of listening is speech

Busiol states that analytic listening is directed toward the unconscious as it appears in speech, not toward a general assessment of personality.

Freud's work on dreams, slips, jokes, and negation provides the foundation. These phenomena are important because intended meaning breaks down and something else becomes audible.

## 6.2 Listen to the letter

Busiol's strongest technical recommendation is to remain close to the exact wording. A patient's unusual phrase can organize a whole chain of associations.

For example, a person who presents himself as serving a lover may describe physically "holding" the lover in a way that also evokes the idiom of having someone under control. The point is not to decide that the idiom reveals the final truth. The point is that the signifier opens a contradiction in the speaker's self-description.

The method is:

- retain the original word;
- do not translate it into the therapist's vocabulary;
- ask what else the word can mean;
- follow the analysand's associations rather than supplying your own;
- notice whether the word repeats elsewhere.

## 6.3 Fundamental rule and free association

The analysand is invited to say what comes to mind without selecting only what seems relevant, coherent, sensible, or socially acceptable.

This is not simply permission to talk freely. It is a method for loosening conscious control over discourse.

## 6.4 Free-floating attention

The analyst's counterpart is evenly suspended attention. The analyst does not decide in advance what is important. Too much concentration on the apparent topic may cause the analyst to miss the signifier that later becomes decisive.

A practical self-analysis version is to review a piece of writing or a remembered conversation and ask:

- Which word did I almost ignore because it seemed incidental?
- Which phrase carries an odd emotional charge?
- Where did I correct myself?
- What did I repeat without noticing?

## 6.5 Associations are retroactive

A word acquires meaning through what follows it. Busiol therefore discourages premature interpretation.

This is a strong methodological discipline for self-analysis: **do not interpret the first association as the answer**. Continue the chain.

## 6.6 Psychoanalysis is not a theory of communication

Ordinary communication ideally reduces ambiguity. Psychoanalysis often works by preserving ambiguity long enough for another meaning to emerge.

This is one of the major differences between psychoanalytic listening and communication-skills approaches.

In couple therapy, both are useful but they solve different problems:

- communication skills help two people exchange information and needs;
- analytic listening asks what else is happening in the choice of words, repetition, misunderstanding, and demand.

## 6.7 The limits of comprehension

Busiol warns that understanding the other can become an imaginary operation in which the listener silently reduces the other to familiar categories.

This does not mean empathy is useless. It means that empathy should not become certainty.

A useful rule is:

> The moment I feel I completely understand what the other means, I should check what I may have excluded.

## 6.8 Make misunderstanding work

The analyst can use equivocation, citation, punctuation, silence, or a question to interrupt a fixed meaning.

The purpose is not confusion for its own sake. The purpose is to loosen a closed narrative.

## 6.9 The L schema

Busiol uses Lacan's L schema to distinguish two axes:

- the imaginary axis between ego and counterpart;
- the symbolic axis between subject and Other.

His clinical contrast includes ordinary dialogue versus free association, intended meaning versus another meaning, ego-centered attention versus free-floating attention, comprehension versus productive misunderstanding, and universal knowledge versus singular knowledge.

This table is useful for couples because many conflicts remain entirely on the imaginary axis: accusation, counter-accusation, comparison, recognition, rivalry, who is right, who is selfish, who understands whom.

A Lacanian intervention asks whether something in the speech can shift the discussion from **me versus you** toward the signifiers and demands structuring both positions.

## 6.10 Interpretation

Busiol presents Lacanian interpretation as closer to citation and enigma than to explanation. A useful interpretation may return the analysand's own word in a new position, emphasize a contradiction, or open a question.

Interpretation should not produce a new dogma.

## 6.11 Solitude

The chapter ends with a distinction between isolation and analytic solitude. Analysis does not aim to eliminate difference by achieving perfect mutual understanding. It can help a subject assume responsibility for speaking in the first person without requiring another person to guarantee meaning.

This is highly relevant to couples. Intimacy does not abolish the fact that each person speaks from a singular relation to language and desire.

# 7. Chapter 3: Desire and enjoyment in psychoanalysis

## 7.1 Desire as the compass

Busiol argues that listening to unconscious desire is what distinguishes psychoanalysis from psychotherapies organized mainly around conscious goals, adaptation, or symptom control.

A slip matters because it may reveal something that does not coincide with the ego's intention.

## 7.2 Desire is not will

The chapter distinguishes desire from conscious wanting. Desire can surprise, embarrass, or contradict the self-image.

Busiol emphasizes several structural features:

- desire does not originate in a sovereign ego;
- desire exceeds need;
- desire depends on lack;
- desire requires limits rather than total possession;
- desire does not automatically conform to moral ideals or conscious values;
- jouissance can operate against desire by fixing the subject in repetition.

**Critical clarification:** saying that unconscious desire is not morally tidy does not mean that every desire should be acted upon. Desire, action, consent, ethics, and law are distinct questions.

## 7.3 Desire of the Other

The Other enters desire in several ways:

- the subject wants what appears desirable to others;
- the subject wants to be desired;
- the subject asks what the Other wants from them;
- family and cultural signifiers pre-exist the subject and shape what can become desirable.

This makes desire alienating. A person may spend years pursuing an inherited ideal before realizing that it does not organize a satisfying life.

## 7.4 Desire arises from lack

Desire is not eliminated by satisfaction because it is not reducible to a concrete need. This is why the fantasy that the right object will produce total completion repeatedly fails.

For couple work, this is crucial. A partner cannot permanently eliminate the subject's lack. Demanding that they do so easily turns love into accusation.

## 7.5 Hysterical desire

Busiol describes hysteria as a discourse organized around dissatisfaction and the desire of the Other. The hysterical subject may seek to provoke desire while resisting closure in a final object or identity.

The clinical risk is to interpret every dissatisfaction as a practical problem to solve. Sometimes dissatisfaction itself is structurally important to the subject's desire.

## 7.6 Obsessional desire

The obsessional position is described as attempting to neutralize desire by delay, control, knowledge, rules, and mental activity. The subject may be preoccupied with what the Other wants while postponing an act that would put their own desire at stake.

For couples, a useful distinction is:

- hysterical strategy: keep the other's desire alive and unsatisfied;
- obsessional strategy: manage the other's desire so that it never becomes too demanding or unpredictable.

These are structural tendencies, not personality labels.

## 7.7 Master and slave

The chapter uses the master-slave dialectic to show how desire can be organized around power, recognition, and dependence. A person may consciously reject authority while repeatedly seeking figures who occupy a master position.

## 7.8 Jouissance

Jouissance is not ordinary pleasure. It refers to satisfaction that can exceed comfort and operate through repetition, suffering, excess, compulsion, or bodily intensity.

A self-analysis question derived from this chapter is:

> What does this pattern allow me to repeat that I might lose if the problem were actually solved?

## 7.9 Castration as limit

Busiol uses symbolic castration to name the limit imposed by language and the impossibility of being, having, or knowing everything.

This is particularly useful in couple work. A partner cannot know everything, provide everything, or guarantee desire. The fantasy that the relationship should remove this limit creates chronic disappointment.

## 7.10 Law and desire

For Busiol, law is not simply the enemy of desire. A limit can make desire possible by preventing total saturation.

This is another useful correction to the idea that freedom equals unrestricted access to satisfaction.

## 7.11 From sentence to narrative

The chapter repeatedly opposes fixed certainty to an unfolding narrative. The analyst listens for metaphors and signifiers that can move the subject from a closed self-description into a new articulation.

## 7.12 Clinical direction

The closing ethical move is central: rather than asking only what others did, ask what part the subject has in the repeated situation.

Busiol uses an example of a man who repeatedly lends money to friends and later feels exploited. The analytic question is not whether the friends behaved well. It is what desire organized the subject's repeated position as educator, rescuer, or benefactor.

This yields one of the book's most useful self-analysis formulations:

> What part do I have in what I repeatedly complain about?

This should never be used to erase abuse, deception, or coercion. It is a question about one's own repetitive participation where such participation genuinely exists.

# 8. Chapter 4: Listening, linguistic precision, and intervention

Giancarlo Calciolari develops a highly idiosyncratic account influenced by Armando Verdiglione's cyphermatics. This chapter should therefore be stored separately from mainstream Lacanian doctrine.

Its clinically useful core is the insistence that the apparently strange linguistic element should not be normalized too quickly.

Calciolari argues that a symptom should be read rather than simply suppressed. A bizarre image, word, or fantasy may be the starting point of an unfolding narrative. If the clinician immediately corrects it according to reality, the patient's own associative path may be closed.

His recurring example concerns a patient's fear of a crocodile under the analytic couch. The point is not the literal truth of the crocodile. The point is that the clinician's task is not exhausted by proving that no crocodile is there.

The chapter's useful principle is:

> The clinically relevant unit is often the linguistic element that appears wrong, absurd, or misplaced.

Calciolari also emphasizes that analytic intervention requires precision and should arise from the structure of the speech rather than from a universal theory imposed on the patient.

### Provenance warning

The chapter's concepts of "logic of the word", tripartition of the sign, mirror-gaze-voice as a special triad, and other cyphermatic notions should be attributed to Calciolari/Verdiglione rather than to Lacan.

# 9. Chapter 5: Lacan in Italy and the institutional problem

This chapter is primarily historical. It maps Lacan's visits to Italy from the 1953 Rome Discourse through his later lectures and attempts to organize Italian Lacanian groups.

Important milestones include:

- 1953: the Rome Discourse;
- 1967: lectures in Naples, Rome, Pisa, and Milan;
- 1969: further Italian appearances;
- 1972-1974: growth of Italian Lacanian groups and Lacan's attempts to coordinate them;
- 1974: the Italian Note and the short-lived project of a common association;
- 1981: Lacan's death and further fragmentation of the field.

Busiol then explains how Italy's 1989 law regulating psychologists and psychotherapy transformed psychoanalytic institutional life. In his account, the law professionalized and standardized training but also complicated the older Lacanian idea that analyst formation is grounded in personal analysis, supervision, study, and a singular path rather than a state-certified curriculum alone.

### Corpus value

This chapter is useful for understanding why Italian Lacanian clinical writing is institutionally diverse and why several contributors do not share the same post-Lacanian lineage.

# 10. Chapter 6: Hysteria, anorexia/bulimia, and contemporary clinical questions

Jean-Luc Cacciali presents hysteria as a structure that changes its social mask rather than disappearing.

## 10.1 Hysteria advances masked

The observable symptoms of hysteria change with the epoch. The analytic task is therefore not to search only for nineteenth-century conversion symptoms but to identify the structure beneath new presentations.

## 10.2 Symptom as message

Cacciali returns to Freud's conversion cases. A bodily symptom can articulate a conflict in language even when it appears somatic.

His Lacanian emphasis is that interpretation should not simply explain the symptom's meaning. It should attend to the signifier through which the subject's position is articulated.

## 10.3 Transference and the supposed knowledge of the analyst

The hysterical subject can place the analyst in the position of someone who knows, then challenge or expose the insufficiency of that knowledge.

This is clinically important because the analyst can easily become recruited into the role of master, expert, rescuer, or moral authority.

## 10.4 Hysterical dissatisfaction

Cacciali emphasizes dissatisfaction as a key position. The danger for the therapist is to take dissatisfaction at face value and attempt to provide the missing object.

The analytic question is what function the dissatisfaction has in relation to desire.

## 10.5 Hysterical neurosis and hysterical discourse

The chapter distinguishes a clinical structure from Lacan's discourse of the hysteric. A person need not have hysterical neurosis to occupy the hysteric's discourse.

This distinction is useful in couple work. A partner may temporarily occupy a hysterical position by addressing the other as one who should know, demanding an answer, and then demonstrating that the answer is inadequate.

## 10.6 Collective hysteria

Cacciali discusses identification and collective forms of hysterical contagion. This is secondary to the couple project but useful when social ideals or group identifications enter a relationship.

## 10.7 The question of femininity

The chapter links hysteria to the question of femininity and to the problem of how a subject locates themselves in relation to sexual difference and the desire of the Other.

The interview with Charles Melman extends this into contemporary questions about parenthood, sexual orientation, and the weakening of traditional paternal reference points. These are **Melman's clinical interpretations**, not neutral empirical conclusions and not direct statements by Lacan.

### Couple relevance

When one partner repeatedly asks the other to define them, reassure them, prove desire, or provide certainty, ask:

- Is the demand actually satisfiable?
- What would happen to desire if the answer were final?
- Is the partner being recruited as a master who must know?
- Does disappointment itself preserve the relational structure?

# 11. Chapter 7: From phobia to obsessive discourse

Gabriele Lodari uses "discourse" rather than rigid diagnostic categories and develops a reading influenced by both Lacan and Verdiglione.

## 11.1 Phobia as an attempt to localize anxiety

Using Little Hans, the chapter presents phobia as a way of giving anxiety an object and a geography. A diffuse and difficult relation to lack can become manageable when danger is attached to a specific thing or place.

This anticipates Marco Focchi's later treatment of panic.

## 11.2 Father as function rather than person

Lodari emphasizes that the paternal function is symbolic and cannot be reduced to whether the biological father is physically present.

## 11.3 From phobia to obsession

The obsessional position is organized around control, classification, knowledge, rules, and the attempt to fix meaning.

## 11.4 Lorenzo's case

The extended case of Lorenzo moves across obsessional, psychotic, and mystical phenomena. The case is clinically useful because it illustrates the book's resistance to reducing a person to a single label.

The treatment emphasizes narrative, wordplay, and the need not to let the analyst become a fixed ideal or master of knowledge.

## 11.5 Obsession and closed space

Lorenzo's attachment to the family home, routines, purification, renunciation, and scrupulous control becomes a model of how obsession can protect against unpredictable encounter.

### Couple relevance

An obsessional couple position may appear as:

- constant analysis of the partner instead of an act;
- insisting on precise rules before commitment;
- using knowledge to avoid vulnerability;
- delaying decisions indefinitely;
- converting desire into administration;
- creating a highly controlled domestic environment in which surprise is minimized.

Again, this is a structural reading of speech and action, not a diagnostic label.

# 12. Chapter 8: Paranoia and the ethics of not forcing meaning

Fabrizio Gambini's chapter is valuable for its technical restraint.

## 12.1 Psychoanalysis is not a total narrative

Gambini warns against turning psychoanalytic theory into a coherent story that explains everything. A total explanation can itself become paranoid in structure because it eliminates contingency and ambiguity.

## 12.2 Après-coup

The chapter stresses Freud's Nachträglichkeit and Lacan's après-coup. Later events can reorganize the meaning of earlier events. Psychic history is therefore not a simple chronological chain.

## 12.3 Diagnosis belongs to formations, not to the whole person

One of the strongest methodological claims in the chapter is that psychoanalytic diagnosis should not collapse the person into a label. A subject with psychotic phenomena can also produce neurotic or ordinary forms of discourse.

The clinical task is to listen to the formation that is present rather than deciding that every word must express a fixed structure.

## 12.4 The analyst's restraint

Gambini describes a patient who spent months speaking about a dream before revealing persecutory fears around the birth of his child. The analyst's refusal to pry prematurely allowed the consulting room to become a place the patient could use during a crisis.

The lesson is straightforward:

> Diagnostic curiosity can become clinically intrusive.

## 12.5 Do not argue a delusion away

Gambini argues that with paranoid delusion the analyst should not try to prove the patient wrong or force an insight into the unconscious. The aim is to keep experience within a web of discourse and avoid escalating persecutory transference.

### Safety and self-analysis note

This chapter is not a guide for managing psychosis alone. If a person is experiencing delusions, hallucinations, severe disorganization, or risk of harm, professional psychiatric and clinical support is required. The self-analysis value of the chapter is methodological: **do not confuse certainty with truth, and do not force an interpretation where the capacity to symbolize is fragile.**

# 13. Chapter 9: Panic as a phenomenon of modernity

Marco Focchi gives one of the clearest clinical chapters in the book.

## 13.1 Listening beyond conscious intention

He begins from Freud's evenly suspended attention. Analytic listening waits beyond the point where ordinary communication would decide the meaning.

## 13.2 Reality and fantasy

An analysis becomes possible when the subject can move from simply reporting external events toward articulating fantasy and the drive dimension of those events.

Focchi is pragmatic about setting. The couch can facilitate this shift, but he rejects dogmatism. Some patients need face-to-face treatment, especially when visible presence provides necessary support.

## 13.3 Panic is not simply a symptom

Focchi distinguishes panic from a classical symptom. Panic is presented as an eruption of the Real, closer to anxiety than to a meaningful symbolic formation.

He also insists that panic is a phenomenon, not a structural diagnosis. It can appear in different clinical structures.

## 13.4 Identification with the diagnostic label

Contemporary symptom labels can provide community and relief, but they can also become narcissistic identities: "I am a panic sufferer", "I am anorexic", "I am an addict".

This differs from Lacan's late notion of identification with the symptom after analytic work. At the beginning, identifying with a label may close inquiry rather than open it.

## 13.5 Anxiety and panic as different temporalities

Focchi offers a useful temporal distinction:

- anxiety signals the imminence of something threatening;
- panic is the moment in which the threat is experienced as already present and overwhelming;
- depression can appear afterward as a relation to an apparently irreversible loss.

## 13.6 Treatment in two modes

Focchi proposes two complementary modes.

**Phobic-geographic mode:** construct symbolic limits and coordinates around the hole so the person can move through the world without falling into unbounded panic.

**Affective-temporal mode:** re-establish a place for lack, mourning, and desire rather than leaving the subject captured by excessive presence.

## 13.7 Initial containment

In acute panic, the first task may be to provide a stable point rather than interpret. Focchi describes the analyst remaining calm and helping the patient establish enough support for symbolic work to become possible later.

### Couple relevance

A partner can easily be recruited as the emergency stabilizer. This may be necessary at times, but if it becomes the only solution, the relationship can be reorganized around panic.

Useful questions include:

- Is the partner becoming the sole guarantee that panic will not occur?
- What situations have become impossible without the partner's presence?
- Can support be provided without turning the couple into a permanent avoidance system?

# 14. Chapter 10: Anorexia, bulimia, binge eating, and obesity

Domenico Cosenza rejects a purely behavioral reading of eating disorders.

## 14.1 The symptom as a solution

A central proposition is that the eating disorder may function as a solution to an otherwise intolerable problem. This does not make it benign. It changes the clinical question from "How do we eliminate the abnormal behavior?" to "What has this behavior been doing for the subject?"

## 14.2 Food is relational from the beginning

Food is never merely nutrition in human life. Feeding is bound to care, demand, desire, satisfaction, refusal, and the Other.

This helps explain why conflicts around food can become conflicts around dependence and autonomy.

## 14.3 All or nothing

Cosenza describes anorexic-bulimic logic as difficulty remaining within gradation. A little can become intolerable because it threatens to become everything. Restriction and bingeing can therefore form two sides of a single relation to excess.

## 14.4 Singularity of rituals

The form of bingeing or restriction matters. Some patients use food indiscriminately, others create highly organized rituals. Psychoanalytic listening asks what singular structure appears in the ritual rather than treating all cases as homogeneous.

## 14.5 The symptom as protection from the Other's demand

An extended case describes anorexia emerging in relation to a maternal demand experienced as excessive. The anorexic solution becomes a way of producing a limit where the subject could not otherwise say no.

This is particularly relevant for self-analysis:

> Which symptom is doing the work of a boundary I cannot state directly?

## 14.6 Clinic of excess and medical risk

Cosenza explicitly states that severe eating disorders require multidisciplinary care. The bodily risk can become extreme, and the subject's relation to jouissance can override conscious intention.

### Safety note

Do not use this chapter to psychologize medical danger. Significant restriction, purging, bingeing, severe weight change, fainting, cardiac symptoms, electrolyte disturbance, or other medical risk requires appropriate medical assessment and treatment. Psychoanalytic meaning and medical safety are separate but complementary issues.

# 15. Chapter 11: Depressions

Franco Lolli treats depression as a heterogeneous phenomenon rather than a single structure.

## 15.1 Depressive phenomenology

Common features include reduced vitality, social withdrawal, self-devaluation, repetitive negative thought, loss of perspective, and a sense that time has stopped.

## 15.2 Loss and self-value in neurosis

Lolli reads neurotic depression through Freud's _Mourning and Melancholia_ and Lacan's account of mourning. The loss of a valued object can expose the subject to their own lack and undermine the fantasy of being valuable or irreplaceable to the Other.

## 15.3 From losing the object to identifying with loss

Depression can preserve the lost object by turning loss itself into a stable position. The subject becomes attached to the loss and may resist the work of mourning because mourning would require allowing absence to become real.

## 15.4 Rejection of castration

Lolli frames some depression as a refusal of the symbolic work of loss. Desire requires lack; if loss cannot be symbolized, desire can become immobilized.

## 15.5 Neurotic and psychotic depression

The chapter distinguishes depressive phenomena occurring in different structures. It should not be read as a single causal theory of depression.

## 15.6 Refusal to know

Lolli uses Lacan's severe formulation of depressive "moral cowardice" to focus on a refusal to know about one's implication and desire. This should be used carefully. It is not a moral judgment on clinically depressed people and should not be turned into blame.

## 15.7 Transference and reanimation

In severe depressive withdrawal, the analyst may need to take a more active role in establishing a libidinal bond before interpretation can function. Lolli speaks of provoking or reanimating transference, then later allowing the analyst to cease occupying the position of privileged object.

## 15.8 Contemporary social discourse

Lolli links depression to a culture that disavows loss and continually promises replacement through consumption. This is an interpretive social thesis, not an empirically settled causal model.

### Couple relevance

Depression can reorganize a couple around rescue, guilt, helplessness, resentment, or the belief that the partner must restore lost vitality.

Useful analytic questions are:

- What loss has become difficult to symbolize?
- What role is the partner asked to occupy in relation to that loss?
- Is the relationship becoming the sole proof of personal value?
- Does rescuing preserve the depressed position as much as it relieves it?

### Safety note

Severe depression, suicidality, inability to function, psychotic symptoms, or inability to maintain basic self-care require professional assessment. The chapter's psychodynamic formulations do not replace medical or psychiatric evaluation.

# 16. Chapter 12: Addictions, toxicomania, and the body as payment

Jean-Louis Chassaing gives the term addiction a striking etymological and juridical frame. He traces it to a relation of debt and enslavement in Roman law and uses this to think about bodily dependence.

## 16.1 Addiction as debt and slavery

The metaphor is clinically productive: a person becomes bound to an object or practice in a way that increasingly uses the body as payment.

## 16.2 Addiction is not one diagnosis

Chassaing emphasizes heterogeneity. Substance dependence, occasional use, compulsive behavior, and different psychiatric structures cannot be collapsed into a single category.

## 16.3 The product is not the whole case

The object matters, but the same substance can occupy very different functions in different subjects. The clinician should ask:

- Which substance or behavior?
- Under what circumstances?
- What does it regulate?
- What precedes use?
- What follows it?
- What relation does the subject have to dependence?
- What medical, legal, social, and family conditions are present?

## 16.4 Team-based treatment

The chapter strongly supports multidisciplinary work in serious addiction. Medical, psychiatric, psychological, social, and sometimes legal dimensions need to be distinguished rather than confused.

## 16.5 Immediate object versus speech

Chassaing interprets contemporary addiction partly through the immediate availability of objects of enjoyment. The addictive object can replace more mediated symbolic processes.

### Couple relevance

Addiction frequently recruits the partner into monitoring, concealment, rescue, punishment, or debt repayment. The psychoanalytic question should not replace the practical one. The couple needs to distinguish:

- the addicted subject's responsibility;
- the partner's own repetitive position;
- medical and safety issues;
- financial and legal consequences;
- what the relationship does to stabilize or destabilize the addictive circuit.

# 17. Chapter 13: Limit states or borderline

Chassaing treats borderline as a difficult and unstable category rather than a clearly defined Lacanian structure.

## 17.1 The category is itself imprecise

The chapter reviews the history of borderline formulations and emphasizes their heterogeneity. It resists assuming that "borderline" names one unified entity.

## 17.2 Clinical tendencies

The chapter describes possible features such as:

- narcissistic fragility;
- intense anxiety;
- depression;
- depersonalization;
- acting out;
- addictions;
- impulsivity;
- self-harm;
- dependence on the other;
- unstable or massive transference;
- difficulty symbolizing experience;
- reliance on action when speech fails.

These are descriptive markers, not a checklist that establishes a diagnosis.

## 17.3 The clinical problem of unstable identity

One of the most useful themes is the sense of being unable to maintain a stable subjective position in relation to others. The other can feel invasive, while separation can produce annihilation anxiety.

## 17.4 Treatment stance

Chassaing emphasizes patience, flexibility, firmness, and vigilance. The therapist should expect strong demands and instability without reducing the case to manipulation or resistance.

### Self-analysis limit

This chapter is poor material for self-diagnosis. The category is contested and the author explicitly treats it as imprecise. Use it to recognize dimensions of experience, not to decide "I am borderline" or "my partner is borderline".

# 18. Chapter 14: The relationship with the mother

Renata Miletto gives a careful Lacanian account of the maternal relation that is directly relevant to adult love and couple life.

## 18.1 The mother appears inside a wider symbolic structure

The mother-child relation is never literally only two people. The mother already has relationships, language, history, desire, and a place within a culture.

This prevents the mother from being treated as the sole causal agent of the subject's later difficulties.

## 18.2 Mother as first Other

For the infant, the maternal figure usually occupies the first position from which care, language, satisfaction, and desire arrive. The child's dependence is therefore simultaneously biological and symbolic.

The first encounter is not just with nourishment but with **the desire of the Other**.

## 18.3 Presence and absence

Subjective development requires the experience that the object is not continuously available. Absence opens a space for representation, search, and desire.

This is a major bridge to couple dynamics. A partner who is required to be continuously present may be recruited to repair an impossible fantasy of uninterrupted satisfaction.

## 18.4 Das Ding

Miletto draws on Seminar VII to describe the Thing as what remains unrepresentable in the first encounter with the Other. The lost object is not simply something once possessed and then lost. It is structurally lost.

This matters because adult love cannot literally restore the primordial object.

## 18.5 Mother as signifier

The real mother and the signifier "mother" must be distinguished. In an adult subject, "mother" may organize meanings such as home, safety, engulfment, perfect love, debt, sacrifice, life, recognition, or demand.

## 18.6 The fantasy of wordless love

One of the most useful observations in the chapter is the association of perfect maternal love with a fantasy that words should not be necessary.

This translates directly into couple conflict:

> If you really loved me, you would already know.

From a Lacanian perspective, this fantasy denies the gap introduced by language. Love that requires no speech becomes an impossible standard.

## 18.7 Pre-Oedipal fusion and paternal function

Miletto describes the paternal function as introducing a third term that interrupts the fantasy of a complete mother-child dyad.

Again, this function should not be reduced to the empirical presence of a male father. It names a symbolic operation of separation and limitation.

## 18.8 "Abandoning the mother"

The chapter's strongest practical point is that analytic separation is not necessarily about physically distancing oneself from one's real mother. It is about relinquishing the fantasy of direct, immediate, certain enjoyment without loss, misunderstanding, or symbolic mediation.

### Couple relevance

Ask:

- Is my partner being asked to function as "mother" in the sense of unconditional availability?
- Do I expect them to know without being told?
- Does distance feel like abandonment rather than ordinary separateness?
- Is my partner required to confirm that I exist, matter, or am lovable?
- Do I punish the partner for introducing difference into what I wanted to experience as a complete dyad?

# 19. Chapter 15: Listening to perversion

Sergio Benvenuto gives an extended single case rather than a general manual of treatment.

## 19.1 Perversion is not a simple diagnostic identity

Benvenuto warns against assuming a pure category. Clinical subjects often show mixed neurotic, psychotic, narcissistic, and perverse features.

## 19.2 Fabio's case

Fabio presents with depression, anxiety, narcissistic fragility, a sense of imposture, serial relationships, and a sexual life organized around masochistic and humiliating scenarios.

A recurring structure is that ordinary possession of the loved object extinguishes desire. Desire is reignited by distance, betrayal, rivalry, or staged humiliation.

## 19.3 Love and desire are split

Fabio can love and respect one woman while directing intense sexual desire elsewhere. The case echoes Freud's account of the debasement of erotic life.

For couple analysis, this is important because it shows that attachment, love, desire, and jouissance do not automatically converge on the same object.

## 19.4 Repetition of a fixed jouissance

Benvenuto emphasizes that the problem is not simply the unusual sexual practice. The clinically significant issue is the rigid repetition of the same route to jouissance.

This is a crucial distinction. The erotic content alone does not establish pathology. Repetition, compulsion, suffering, lack of regard for the other's subjectivity, and inability to vary the relation are more analytically important.

## 19.5 The analyst as one who does not know

Fabio rejects the analyst as expert but can use the analyst as a witness and companion who does not pretend to know him completely. Benvenuto therefore accepts a transferential position of acknowledged ignorance.

## 19.6 Interpretation and accompaniment

Benvenuto explicitly departs from a rigid anti-transference-interpretation stance. He argues that transference can sometimes be interpreted but should not become the explanation for everything the patient says.

### Couple relevance

This chapter is useful when desire in a relationship depends on:

- distance;
- rivalry;
- humiliation;
- danger of loss;
- third parties;
- being desired by others;
- staged asymmetry;
- taboo or transgression.

The analytic question is not "Is this normal?" but:

- What structure of desire is required for excitement?
- What role must the partner occupy?
- Can the partner remain a subject or are they reduced to a prop in the fantasy?
- Is the arrangement consensual?
- What happens when the fantasy can no longer be staged?

Consent, safety, and non-coercion remain prior requirements.

# 20. Chapter 16: Recalcati's clinic of the void

This is one of the most important post-Lacanian chapters, but it must be clearly attributed to Massimo Recalcati.

## 20.1 Clinic of lack versus clinic of void

Recalcati contrasts two clinical configurations.

**Clinic of lack:** desire, repression, return of the repressed, symptom as metaphor, divided subject, transferential question.

**Clinic of void:** weak access to desire, narcissistic fragility, anxiety, compulsive jouissance, acting out, and symptoms that function more as solutions or identity supports than as enigmas.

He does not present the clinic of void as a new formal structure equivalent to neurosis or psychosis.

## 20.2 The void is not the same as lack

Lack can animate desire. Void names an experience in which lack has become disconnected from desire and is lived as emptiness, anxiety, or failure of existence.

## 20.3 New symptoms as anti-love

Recalcati argues that addictions, eating disorders, and other contemporary symptoms can function as non-human partners that reduce dependence on the Other.

The object becomes a remedy for anxiety rather than a cause of desire.

## 20.4 Symptom as identity

The contemporary symptom can stabilize the ego rather than divide it. If the person becomes "an anorexic", "an addict", or another fixed identity, the symptom may provide coherence.

This creates a major technical difficulty: why would a subject give up the symptom if it is what holds them together?

## 20.5 The problem of transference

Classical psychoanalysis assumes that the symptom raises a question addressed to someone supposed to know. In the clinic of void, that movement may not occur. The transferential investment may instead remain attached to the body, substance, food, image, or ritual.

## 20.6 Preliminary treatment of the demand

Recalcati argues that before interpretation can work, the analyst may need to transform the demand for cure into a subjective question.

## 20.7 Rectifying the Other

His most distinctive technical proposal is that some contemporary patients first require an experience of an Other different from the traumatic Other they have known. The analyst may need to offer reliability, holding, and a non-invasive relation before asking the subject to assume responsibility in the classical analytic sense.

This is a significant departure from caricatures of Lacanian treatment as cold silence.

### Couple relevance

The clinic of void provides a way to think about relationships in which the partner is used less as a desired Other and more as a regulatory object.

Questions include:

- Is the partner desired, or mainly used to prevent anxiety or emptiness?
- Does separation threaten not just loneliness but loss of subjective consistency?
- Is the relationship organized around constant reassurance, monitoring, or fusion?
- Are non-human objects such as work, food, substances, screens, pornography, exercise, or shopping functioning as alternative partners?

# 21. Chapter 17: Good and bad encounters with jealousy

Paule Cacciali's short chapter returns jealousy to early identification.

## 21.1 Jealousy is not only rivalry

Using the young child and sibling relation, the chapter emphasizes that jealousy also involves identification with the rival.

The rival is not simply hated. The subject is fascinated by the other's position and may want to occupy it.

## 21.2 Jealousy and the mirror

Jealousy belongs to the imaginary formation of the ego. The other child is simultaneously model, rival, double, and competitor.

## 21.3 Introduction of a third

Jealousy becomes structurally important because it introduces a third position into what might otherwise be imagined as a closed mother-child dyad.

## 21.4 Good and bad outcomes

The rival can be integrated into a social relation in which competition and recognition coexist, or the situation can regress toward an "either you or me" logic in which the rival must disappear.

### Couple relevance

Adult jealousy can therefore be analyzed on at least four levels:

- fear of losing the loved object;
- rivalry with the third person;
- identification with the rival;
- destabilization of the subject's own identity.

A useful question is not only:

> What does the rival have that I fear losing?

but also:

> What position does the rival embody that I desire or cannot tolerate in myself?

# 23. Concept glossary for retrieval, with provenance controls

The book contains both standard Lacanian terms and formulations specific to particular contributors. The provenance labels below are important because the volume is plural rather than doctrinally uniform.

## Analysand

The person engaged in analysis, emphasizing activity rather than passive receipt of treatment. Busiol repeatedly contrasts a patient who wants a symptom removed with an analysand who begins to hear something unexpected in their own speech.

**Self-analysis use:** ask not only "What do I want fixed?" but "What in my own speech or repetition has become a question for me?"

## Analyst's desire

A technical Lacanian notion, not the therapist's personal wish for a particular outcome. It names the position that sustains analytic work rather than gratifying demand, supplying an ideal, or directing the patient toward the analyst's preferred life.

**Do not reduce to:** caring, motivation, empathy, or the therapist's conscious goals.

## Castration

In the book's glossary and several chapters, castration refers to the effect of symbolic law and language that installs structural lack and limits complete satisfaction.

**Clinical function:** makes desire possible by preventing the fantasy of total completion.

**Couple relevance:** many conflicts can be read as attempts to make the partner abolish lack, uncertainty, or limitation.

## Clinic of lack

Recalcati's retrospective label for classical neurotic configurations organized around desire, repression, division, and symptom formation.

**Provenance:** Recalcati, post-Lacanian formulation. Not one of Lacan's formal diagnostic structures.

## Clinic of the void

Recalcati's term for contemporary presentations in which emptiness, identity fragility, bodily enactment, compulsive jouissance, and non-human objects can predominate over a decipherable neurotic question.

**Provenance:** Recalcati. Do not attribute the phrase directly to Lacan.

## Demand

A need articulated in language becomes more than a request for an object. Demand carries a demand for recognition or love from the Other.

**Couple use:** separate the concrete request from the additional proof of love or value expected to accompany compliance.

## Desire

Desire is not simply conscious wanting. It emerges from lack, is mediated by language and the Other, and often appears indirectly through symptoms, fantasy, repetition, slips, and contradictions.

**Retrieval distinction:** need ≠ demand ≠ desire.

## Desire of the Other

One of the central Lacanian formulations developed throughout the book. It can refer to desire as structured through the Other and to the subject's question about what the Other wants from them.

**Couple question:** "What do I believe my partner wants from me, and what position does that belief make me occupy?"

## Equivocation

The capacity of a word, sound, phrase, or signifier to support more than one meaning. Analytic listening does not rush to eliminate ambiguity. It uses ambiguity to expose another possible line in the subject's speech.

## Enjoyment / jouissance

A form of satisfaction that cannot be reduced to pleasure. It can persist in repetitive, painful, excessive, or self-defeating arrangements.

**Self-analysis question:** "What satisfaction persists in this scene even though consciously I want it to stop?"

## Fantasy / phantasm

Not mere daydreaming. Fantasy organizes the subject's relation to desire, the Other, and an object. It can provide a recurrent scene in which the subject occupies a particular position.

**Couple use:** identify the repeated relational scenario rather than treating every argument as a new event.

## Fading

A term associated with the divided subject and the fantasy relation. The subject cannot be fully present as a transparent, self-identical entity in the signifying chain.

## Free association

The analysand speaks with reduced censorship rather than delivering only an edited account of what seems relevant.

**Notebook use:** preserve odd details and apparently irrelevant transitions before organizing the material.

## Free-floating attention

Freud's corresponding recommendation for analytic listening. The listener does not decide in advance which part is important and does not strain to remember selectively.

**Practical implication:** do not read a couple transcript only for the declared topic of the argument. Track repetitions, transitions, exact phrases, slips, jokes, metaphors, and sudden affective changes.

## Gaze

A form of object _a_, not simply the act of looking. It concerns the point from which the subject experiences themselves as seen and implicated in the visual field.

## Hole

In the book's glossary, the hole belongs to the Real and is irreducible to symbolic representation.

**Do not equate automatically with:** psychological emptiness, Recalcati's void, or ordinary lack.

## Imaginary

The register of images, ego identifications, similarity, rivalry, mirroring, and dual relations.

**Couple relevance:** competition, comparison, jealousy, demands for sameness, and "me versus you" struggles often have a strong imaginary dimension.

## Interpretation

In the book's clinical orientation, interpretation is not primarily an explanatory speech in which the analyst tells the patient what the symptom means. It may function through punctuation, citation, equivocation, surprise, or an intervention that reopens what appeared settled.

## Jealousy

Cacciali emphasizes that jealousy contains identification with the rival as well as competition. The rival can embody a position the subject both rejects and desires.

## Lack

A structural term. Lack is not merely deprivation of a needed object. It is constitutive of desire and cannot finally be filled by obtaining the correct partner, object, status, or reassurance.

## Letter

Busiol's "listening to the letter" stresses exact linguistic material. A synonym is not analytically equivalent simply because ordinary communication treats it as close enough.

## Little other

The specular counterpart, rival, similar other, or ego-level partner in the imaginary relation.

**Do not confuse with:** the big Other.

## Big Other

The locus of language, law, signifiers, expectations, and symbolic coordinates in which the subject is constituted. A concrete partner may embody or be addressed as the Other in a scene, but the Other is not simply another person.

## Mother as Other

Miletto stresses that "mother" in analytic work cannot be reduced to the biological person. The maternal function concerns an early relation to an Other of care, demand, presence, absence, speech, and desire.

## New symptoms

A broad, contested post-Lacanian label for presentations such as panic, addictions, eating disorders, identity fragility, and bodily or compulsive symptoms that may initially show little transferential curiosity or symbolic articulation.

**Caution:** descriptive category, not a single formal structure.

## Object _a_

The object cause of desire, not an ordinary object that would finally satisfy desire. The book uses the concept in relation to gaze, voice, fantasy, and enjoyment.

## Other's desire

The enigmatic desire attributed to the Other. The question "What does the Other want from me?" is particularly useful in couple material where one partner becomes preoccupied with reading, predicting, or controlling the other's desire.

## Panic

Focchi treats panic as an abrupt event in which ordinary symbolic localization fails and the Real becomes pressing. The diagnostic name itself should not be allowed to become the whole identity of the subject.

## Paternal function

A symbolic function that introduces mediation, separation, limit, and a third term. It is not reducible to the physical presence, sex, authority, or personality of a biological father.

## Phallus

A signifier linked to lack and desire, not the anatomical penis. In this book's glossary it is treated as central to the symbolic organization of lack and the imagined object that would answer it.

## Real

Not ordinary reality. The Real refers to what resists or escapes symbolization and returns as an impasse.

## Rectification of the Other

Recalcati's technical proposal for some contemporary cases. Before asking the subject to assume responsibility in the classical way, the treatment may need to establish an Other that is reliable, bounded, and non-invasive.

**Provenance:** Recalcati, not a general synonym for Lacanian treatment.

## Signifier

A linguistic element whose effects depend on its differential relations to other signifiers. The analytic subject is represented in signifying chains rather than transparently expressing a pre-existing interior meaning.

## Symptom

Across the book, symptom has several functions. It can be a formation of the unconscious, a compromise, a source of jouissance, a message, a stabilizing construction, a bodily enactment, or an identity support. The correct question is therefore not only "How do I remove it?" but "What function does it perform in this case?"

## Subject

The Lacanian subject is not identical with the ego, personality, conscious self, or biological individual. It appears through divisions, contradictions, gaps, and effects of the signifier.

## Subject supposed to know

A key dimension of transference. Analysis becomes possible when knowledge is attributed to the analytic situation or analyst, but the analyst should not occupy the position as an all-knowing expert who explains the subject to themselves.

## Transference

Not merely feelings transferred from the past. In Lacanian practice it is structurally linked to knowledge, desire, and the position of the analyst.

## Void

In Recalcati's usage, an experience of emptiness or lack of subjective consistency that is not productively linked to desire. It should not be collapsed into Lacan's structural lack or the Real hole.

# 24. A Lacanian self-analysis protocol derived from this volume

This is a disciplined reflection tool, not a substitute for analysis. The central constraint is that one should work from one's own speech rather than use theory to explain the other person.

## Step 1: Record the material before interpreting it

Write the sequence of what happened as literally as possible.

Preserve:

- exact phrases;
- unusual words;
- jokes;
- slips;
- repeated expressions;
- sudden changes of topic;
- bodily sensations;
- dreams or images that followed;
- moments when you felt compelled to act;
- what you omitted and later remembered.

Do not improve the language for readability. Busiol's strongest methodological contribution is that paraphrase can erase the very signifier worth hearing.

## Step 2: Separate statement from enunciation

Ask two different questions:

1. What am I explicitly saying?
2. What position do I occupy in saying it this way?

For example, "I only want them to be honest" may be a statement about honesty while the enunciation repeatedly places the speaker as examiner, judge, excluded person, betrayed person, or one who must know.

Do not treat the second level as the hidden "true meaning". It is another dimension of the speech.

## Step 3: Circle the signifiers with charge

Identify words that recur or feel unusually loaded.

Examples:

- respect;
- selfish;
- normal;
- loyal;
- safe;
- freedom;
- trapped;
- enough;
- needy;
- cold;
- impossible;
- proper;
- strong;
- weak;
- honest;
- abandoned.

Then ask:

- Who else in my history used this word?
- What identity does it assign me?
- What opposite identity does it assign the other?
- What happens if I replace it with a synonym? Does the emotional structure change?

If the word cannot be replaced without losing something, the exact signifier matters.

## Step 4: Distinguish need, demand, and desire

Write three lines.

**Need:** What concrete thing is required?

**Demand:** What am I asking the other to give me in addition to the object itself, such as proof of love, recognition, priority, certainty, or reassurance?

**Desire:** What remains unresolved even if the explicit demand is met?

Example:

- Need: an agreed time to talk.
- Demand: prove I matter enough for you to make time immediately.
- Desire: may remain caught in the question of whether I can ever occupy a secure place in the other's desire.

## Step 5: Formulate the question of the Other's desire

Complete:

> I imagine the other wants me to be...

and:

> What I cannot tolerate not knowing about their desire is...

Common positions include indispensable, admired, sexually wanted, obedient, autonomous, grateful, guilty, dependent, jealous, successful, wounded, rescuable, or impossible to possess.

The aim is not to discover what the other actually wants. It is to hear the fantasy through which you interpret their desire.

## Step 6: Locate the recurring fantasy scene

Describe the interaction as if it were a small theatre scene.

Who is:

- demanding?
- withholding?
- watching?
- being watched?
- knowing?
- kept ignorant?
- choosing?
- rejected?
- rescuing?
- accused?
- humiliated?
- desired by a third?
- leaving?
- begging the other not to leave?

Then ask:

> In how many apparently different situations do I reproduce this same distribution of positions?

## Step 7: Ask what satisfaction persists in the symptom

Do not ask only what the pattern costs. Ask what it supplies.

Possible forms of jouissance include:

- certainty;
- moral innocence;
- indignation;
- being indispensable;
- being the betrayed one;
- rescue;
- punishment;
- humiliation;
- sexual intensity;
- endless analysis instead of action;
- control;
- proof that the Other is unreliable;
- permission not to choose;
- being desired only when unavailable.

This question is intentionally uncomfortable. It is not a moral accusation. It explains why conscious intention is often too weak to end a repetitive pattern.

## Step 8: Locate avoidance, postponement, and mastery

For obsessional patterns, ask:

- What decision is continually deferred?
- What additional information do I insist I need before acting?
- Which rule would supposedly guarantee that I cannot make the wrong choice?
- Does thinking function as an alternative to desiring?
- Do I preserve desire by keeping the desired object at a safe distance?

For phobic patterns, ask:

- Which place, person, object, or condition has become the carrier of anxiety?
- What happens if that external organizer is removed?

## Step 9: Examine the relation to dissatisfaction

For hysterical dynamics, ask:

- Do I require the other to desire me while making their satisfaction impossible?
- Does disappointment keep desire alive?
- Do I repeatedly produce an authority and then expose their inadequacy?
- Do I ask the other to tell me who I am and then reject their answer?
- Is the complaint also the means by which the relation continues?

These are structural questions, not labels such as "I am a hysteric" or "my partner is hysterical".

## Step 10: Identify the point where lack becomes intolerable

Ask what limit you are trying to abolish.

Examples:

- not knowing what the other thinks;
- not being continuously desired;
- accepting that love does not guarantee sexual synchrony;
- accepting that no decision is risk-free;
- accepting that the partner has a separate history and fantasy;
- accepting that grief cannot be instantly repaired;
- accepting that one cannot be everything for the other.

This is where castration is clinically useful as a structural term: the problem is often not that something is missing and can be obtained, but that complete closure is unavailable in principle.

## Step 11: Listen for the body and the act

When speech thins out, note what the body or action does.

Examples:

- bingeing;
- restricting;
- compulsive exercise;
- drinking or using substances;
- compulsive sexual activity;
- scrolling;
- shopping;
- panic;
- disappearing from the relationship;
- breaking objects;
- self-harm;
- sudden threats of separation.

The analytic question is what function the act has when symbolization fails. Serious self-harm, violence, eating disorders, substance dependence, or acute psychiatric symptoms require professional assessment rather than self-interpretation alone.

## Step 12: Distinguish lack from void

Ask whether the state feels like:

**Lack:** something missing that sets desire in motion, however painfully;

or:

**Void:** deadness, non-existence, formless emptiness, or a need for an object or person simply to feel held together.

This distinction is Recalcati's, not a formal diagnosis. Its value is practical: a person in a void-like state may need stabilization and reliable relational presence before sophisticated interpretation becomes useful.

## Step 13: Ask what the symptom stabilizes

Complete:

> If this symptom disappeared tomorrow, what problem would become more exposed?

For example, the symptom may currently supply:

- identity;
- routine;
- belonging;
- a boundary against intrusion;
- distance from sexuality;
- a reason not to decide;
- a way to regulate affect;
- a social group;
- a way to avoid dependence on people.

## Step 14: End with a question, not a verdict

A productive self-analysis note should finish with a sharper question rather than a diagnosis.

Examples:

- Why must love be proven through immediate availability for me?
- Why does my desire intensify when the other becomes unavailable?
- What do I preserve by never deciding?
- Why is being misunderstood more tolerable than asking directly?
- What part of the rival do I want for myself?
- Why does certainty about the other's motive calm me even when the certainty is painful?

# 25. A couple-therapy formulation template using Busiol's volume

This template is designed to keep a Lacanian orientation without pretending that the couple can be reduced to a single shared unconscious structure. Each partner remains a subject with a distinct relation to language, desire, fantasy, and jouissance.

## A. The manifest complaint

State the complaint in each partner's own words.

Do not harmonize them prematurely. The difference between the two formulations may itself be important.

## B. Repeated signifiers

For each partner, record recurring words used to name:

- self;
- partner;
- relationship;
- sex;
- loyalty;
- conflict;
- family;
- money;
- work;
- freedom;
- responsibility.

Preserve exact wording.

## C. The demand addressed to the partner

What does each partner explicitly ask for?

Then ask what proof is supposed to accompany fulfillment of that request.

Examples:

- "Tell me where you are" may carry a demand for certainty of love.
- "Give me space" may carry a demand that the other desire without making demands.
- "Have sex with me" may carry a demand to restore narcissistic value.
- "Talk to me" may carry a demand that the other eliminate all opacity.

## D. The question of desire

What question about the other's desire repeatedly appears?

Examples:

- Am I still wanted?
- What am I to you?
- Why do you want someone or something else?
- What do you expect me to be?
- Would you choose me if you were free?
- Why can I not satisfy you?

## E. Imaginary dyad

Where does the relationship collapse into rivalry, mirroring, comparison, or a fight over who is right?

Look for:

- scorekeeping;
- imitation;
- competition over suffering;
- who works harder;
- who sacrifices more;
- sexual comparison;
- jealousy;
- attempts to make the partner identical to oneself.

## F. Symbolic coordinates

What signifiers from family, culture, religion, class, gender expectations, or prior relationships organize the couple?

Examples:

- proper marriage;
- provider;
- good mother;
- loyal husband;
- independent woman;
- successful couple;
- soulmate;
- open-minded;
- traditional;
- strong;
- emotionally available.

The issue is not whether the signifier is socially good or bad. The issue is what position it creates for each subject.

## G. Fantasy scene

Describe the recurrent couple scene without judging it.

Examples:

- one pursues, one disappears;
- one confesses, one interrogates;
- one rescues, one collapses;
- one becomes desirable to a third, the other becomes jealous;
- one criticizes, the other proves inadequacy;
- one withholds sex, the other intensifies demand;
- one is the competent adult, the other the irresponsible child.

Then ask what each partner obtains from the scene.

## H. Jouissance

Where does repetitive satisfaction exceed ordinary pleasure?

A fight that both partners hate may still produce intensity, certainty, punishment, sexualization, or familiar identity positions.

## I. Obsessional and hysterical positions

Do not diagnose personalities. Instead identify moments in discourse.

**Obsessional moves may include:** delay, rule-making, intellectualization, mastery, waiting until desire is safe, rendering the other dependent while protecting oneself from dependence.

**Hysterical moves may include:** sustaining the other's desire through dissatisfaction, demanding an answer about identity, producing a master in order to expose the master's inadequacy, making oneself the cause of the other's desire while preventing closure.

Both positions can occur in either partner and may alternate.

## J. The third and jealousy

If a rival, child, work, parent, friend, ex-partner, pornography, phone, or career enters the scene, ask what function the third performs.

For jealousy, examine not only the fear of loss but identification with the rival:

> What does the rival seem allowed to be or have that I cannot permit myself?

## K. Sex and fantasy

Separate frequency from structure.

Ask:

- Under what conditions does desire appear?
- Does desire require distance?
- Does reassurance reduce erotic charge?
- Does conflict increase erotic charge?
- Is a third party needed in fantasy?
- Does one partner need to feel pursued, refused, admired, humiliated, or irreplaceable?
- What demand for love is being made through sex?

No sexual practice should be pathologized merely because it is unconventional. Consent, autonomy, safety, and capacity to refuse remain basic conditions.

## L. The partner as regulator

Does one partner function as an emergency object against panic, emptiness, or disorganization?

Signs may include:

- inability to tolerate delayed replies;
- constant checking;
- catastrophic reaction to ordinary distance;
- repeated threats of collapse when the other leaves;
- expectation that the partner regulate eating, substances, sleep, or mood;
- replacing broader social and symbolic supports with one person.

This does not itself establish a diagnosis. It identifies a function the relationship may be carrying.

## M. The symptom's protective function

Before trying to eliminate a couple symptom, ask what it prevents.

Sexlessness, chronic arguing, constant messaging, avoidance, jealousy, or overwork may protect against another encounter such as vulnerability, mourning, uncertainty, dependence, difference, or sexual failure.

## N. The intervention test

A useful intervention should open speech rather than settle the case.

Prefer questions that make an exact signifier audible:

- "You used 'trapped' three times. Trapped by what?"
- "You say you need honesty, but what would count as enough honesty?"
- "You said both 'I want him to choose' and 'I don't want him to need me.'"
- "When you say 'normal couple', whose normal is that?"

Avoid turning psychoanalytic vocabulary into accusations such as "you are projecting", "that is your jouissance", or "you are hysterical".

# 26. How this volume complements Mary Morgan's _Couple Relations_

The two books are unusually productive together because they start from different metapsychologies and therefore notice different things.

## 26.1 Morgan asks what the couple creates between two people

Morgan's strongest concepts are relational:

- shared unconscious fantasy;
- couple projective system;
- unconscious belief;
- narcissistic pact;
- interference;
- alterity;
- couple state of mind;
- relational third.

These concepts make recurrent interactional systems visible.

## 26.2 Busiol's volume asks what each subject's speech reveals

The Lacanian volume gives more weight to:

- exact signifiers;
- enunciation;
- the Other;
- desire and demand;
- fantasy;
- jouissance;
- structural positions in discourse;
- lack and castration;
- the analyst's intervention.

This prevents the couple from becoming the only unit of explanation.

## 26.3 A combined reading protocol

When analysing a recurring conflict, use the two levels sequentially.

### Morgan level

Ask:

- What pattern are we co-creating?
- What affect does each partner carry?
- What shared belief governs the couple?
- What shared defence preserves the arrangement?
- Where does the actual other's difference interfere with the script?

### Lacanian level

Then ask separately for each partner:

- Which exact signifiers organize the complaint?
- What demand is addressed to the partner?
- What question about the Other's desire appears?
- What fantasy position repeats?
- What jouissance makes the pattern sticky?
- What lack is the partner being asked to eliminate?

### Why both levels matter

Morgan is stronger at describing the **system between partners**. Busiol and the Lacanian contributors are stronger at preventing the relation from being reduced to a symmetrical system by returning to the **division and desire of each subject**.

The combined approach is particularly useful when a conflict is genuinely co-created but the partners' unconscious positions within it are not equivalent.

# 27. Common interpretive errors when using this volume for self-analysis

## 27.1 Replacing ordinary moral accusations with psychoanalytic accusations

Saying "you are enjoying this" can be just as defensive as saying "you are selfish".

Jouissance should first be asked about oneself.

## 27.2 Treating every striking word as a magic key

Listening to the signifier does not mean free-associating wildly to every word. A signifier becomes clinically important through repetition, context, affect, associative chains, or an unexpected effect on the speaker.

## 27.3 Believing the unconscious is a hidden dictionary

The task is not to decode a universal symbolic meaning. A dream, slip, or symptom acquires significance through the subject's associations.

## 27.4 Using structural terms as personality labels

Hysteric, obsessional, psychotic, or perverse are not insults and should not be assigned to a partner from ordinary behavior.

In particular:

- jealousy does not equal paranoia;
- control does not automatically equal obsessional neurosis;
- unconventional sexuality does not equal perversion;
- intense emotion does not equal borderline structure;
- emptiness does not automatically equal clinic of the void.

## 27.5 Treating desire as an instruction to do whatever one wants

Lacanian desire is not impulsive authenticity. "Following desire" does not mean acting out every wish regardless of consequences, ethics, commitment, or the other person's autonomy.

## 27.6 Treating castration as resignation

Accepting structural lack is not the same as tolerating avoidable neglect, coercion, deception, or abuse. Some deficits are contingent and can be addressed directly.

## 27.7 Assuming every painful repetition is secretly wanted

Jouissance does not mean the subject consciously wants suffering. It identifies a satisfaction embedded in a repetitive organization that may coexist with genuine pain.

## 27.8 Over-reading childhood at the expense of present reality

A present partner can actually lie, withdraw, desire someone else, be incompatible, or behave coercively. Analysis of fantasy does not invalidate external facts.

## 27.9 Making the partner the analyst

A couple should not become a permanent interpretive surveillance system in which each person analyzes the other's slips, childhood, desire, or jouissance.

The practical use is to improve one's own questioning and, where mutually agreed, to make patterns discussable.

## 27.10 Ignoring safety because a dynamic seems theoretically interesting

Violence, coercive control, sexual coercion, serious substance dependence, self-harm, severe eating disorders, psychosis, and acute suicidality require appropriate professional and safety responses. Interpretation is secondary to immediate risk management.

# 28. High-value primary-source cross-reading sequence

Because the book is explicitly Lacanian but multi-authored, its claims should be triangulated against Lacan's own texts already present in the corpus.

## 28.1 Listening, speech, signifier, and intervention

**Read with:** _Écrits_, especially "The Function and Field of Speech and Language in Psychoanalysis", "The Instance of the Letter in the Unconscious", and "The Direction of the Treatment and the Principles of Its Power".

**Question:** what makes an analytic intervention different from advice, explanation, suggestion, or empathic confirmation?

## 28.2 Demand and desire

**Read with:** _Seminar V: Formations of the Unconscious_ and _Seminar VI: Desire and Its Interpretation_.

**Question:** what remains of desire after a demand is satisfied, and how does the subject position themselves in relation to the Other's desire?

## 28.3 Love and transference

**Read with:** _Seminar VIII: Transference_ and Bruce Fink's "Freud and Lacan on Love".

**Question:** what is being loved in the other, what is attributed to them, and how do knowledge and desire enter the bond?

## 28.4 Anxiety, panic, and object _a_

**Read with:** _Seminar X: Anxiety_.

**Question:** when anxiety becomes intense, what has happened to lack and to the subject's relation to the desire of the Other?

## 28.5 Repetition and symptom

**Read with:** _Seminar XI: The Four Fundamental Concepts of Psychoanalysis_ and Freud's _Beyond the Pleasure Principle_.

**Question:** what repeats beyond conscious intention, and what satisfaction is bound to the repetition?

## 28.6 Discourse and contemporary symptoms

**Read with:** _Seminar XVII: The Other Side of Psychoanalysis_ and the Clemens/Grigg collection on Seminar XVII.

**Question:** how do master, university, hysteric, and analyst discourses organize different social and clinical positions, and how should contemporary claims about the capitalist discourse be distinguished from the four formal discourses?

## 28.7 Sexual difference and the failure of complementarity

**Read with:** _Seminar XX: Encore_, Colette Soler's _What Lacan Said About Women_, and Alenka Zupančič's _What Is Sex?_.

**Question:** what demand for natural fit or completion is being imposed on sex and love?

## 28.8 Late symptom and singular stabilization

**Read with:** _Seminar XXIII: The Sinthome_.

**Question:** when is a symptom not merely something to decode and remove but a singular way of knotting a life together?

This final cross-read is especially relevant to Recalcati's claim that some contemporary symptoms function as identity supports or solutions.

# 29. Notebook prompts for self-analysis and couple material

The following prompts are designed to force source discrimination and avoid generic psychoanalytic answers.

- "Analyse this transcript using Busiol's method of listening to the letter. Identify repeated signifiers, equivocations, abrupt transitions, and contradictions. Do not infer motives that are not supported by the speech."
- "Separate need, demand, and desire in each partner's statements. Use Lacan's concepts rather than ordinary relationship advice."
- "What question about the Other's desire is implicit in each partner's complaint? Quote only short phrases from the transcript itself."
- "Identify possible fantasy positions in this recurrent interaction. Present them as hypotheses, not diagnoses."
- "Where might jouissance be located in the repetition? Explain what recurrent satisfaction could persist even though the scene is consciously unwanted."
- "Read this conflict first at the Imaginary level of rivalry and mirroring, then at the Symbolic level of signifiers and demands, and finally identify what seems resistant to symbolization."
- "Does this interaction show an obsessional strategy of delay/mastery, a hysterical strategy of sustaining dissatisfaction, both, or neither? Use discourse positions, not personality labels."
- "What exact relationship signifiers organize this couple: for example loyal, selfish, proper, free, needy, strong, safe? Trace how their meanings differ for the two partners."
- "Analyse jealousy here using Cacciali's emphasis on identification with the rival. What position of the rival may be desired as well as feared?"
- "Analyse this sexual impasse using fantasy, demand for love, desire, and jouissance. Do not assume that frequency of sex directly measures relationship quality."
- "Using Miletto's chapter, identify whether a demand for maternal-style presence, mind-reading, or unconditional availability is being addressed to the partner. Keep the biological mother separate from the symbolic maternal function."
- "Using Focchi, distinguish anxiety, panic, and post-loss depressive experience in this account. Do not diagnose."
- "Using Recalcati, compare a clinic-of-lack formulation with a clinic-of-void formulation. State clearly that these are post-Lacanian heuristic categories, not formal DSM diagnoses or Lacan's own structures."
- "What function does the symptom perform before proposing how it might change? Could it be a message, a source of jouissance, a boundary, an identity support, or a stabilizing solution?"
- "Compare this case using Morgan's couple projective system and Busiol's Lacanian framework. Keep the theoretical vocabularies separate and explain what each reveals that the other misses."
- "What would an analytic question sound like here if the goal were to open speech rather than settle who is right?"

# 30. Highest-value ideas to preserve in the research corpus

If this source must later be compressed, retain these propositions.

1. **Psychoanalysis is as much a listening cure as a talking cure.** The specificity lies in listening beyond intended communication to formations of the unconscious.
2. **Exact wording matters.** Synonyms can erase analytic material because the signifier operates through sound, association, ambiguity, and repetition.
3. **Understanding too quickly can be a resistance.** Analytic listening leaves room for misunderstanding, equivocation, and retroactive meaning.
4. **The patient can learn to listen differently to their own speech.** The transition toward analysand involves becoming surprised by what one says.
5. **Desire is not conscious will.** It is mediated by language, lack, fantasy, and the Other.
6. **Demand always exceeds the requested object.** In intimate life it often carries a demand for love, recognition, certainty, or priority.
7. **Jouissance explains why painful repetitions persist.** The symptom can carry satisfaction beyond ordinary pleasure.
8. **The symptom is not always merely a coded message.** It may also regulate affect, provide identity, create a boundary, or stabilize a precarious subjective organization.
9. **Clinical structure cannot be inferred from surface behavior alone.** The same behavior may have different functions in different subjects.
10. **Contemporary symptoms often present through the body, action, consumption, or identity before they become questions in speech.** This may require modified preliminary clinical work.
11. **The partner is often addressed as an embodiment of the Other.** Couple conflict can therefore involve attempts to know, control, satisfy, escape, or complete the Other's desire.
12. **Jealousy includes identification with the rival.** The rival may embody a desired position rather than merely threaten possession of the loved object.
13. **The maternal and paternal functions are symbolic functions, not simple descriptions of mother and father as persons.**
14. **Analysis should not install the analyst as a master who already knows.** The strongest clinical chapters repeatedly emphasize restraint, singularity, and invention.
15. **Lacanian practice is not uniform.** The volume itself documents substantial differences between schools, generations, and analysts who all work under a Lacanian reference.

# 32. Final synthesis

_Lacanian Psychoanalysis in Practice_ is substantially more central to this research corpus than a generic introductory clinical text because it addresses the point where Lacanian theory is most easily distorted: **what an analyst actually listens to and what an intervention is trying to change**.

The volume's most important practical lesson is methodological. The analytic object is not the polished autobiographical story, the clinician's explanatory model, or the partner's presumed personality. It is the subject as something that appears through speech, repetition, contradiction, equivocation, symptom, fantasy, and jouissance.

For self-analysis, this changes the basic question. Instead of asking only:

> Why am I like this?

or:

> What is wrong with my partner?

one can ask:

> What do I keep saying, what position do I keep occupying when I say it, what do I demand from the Other, what desire remains unsatisfied, and what satisfaction is preserved by the repetition?

For couple work, the book supplies a useful correction to purely interactional models. A couple certainly develops recurrent systems, but the two partners are not interchangeable components of one machine. Each addresses the other through a different history of signifiers, a different fantasy, a different relation to lack, and a different mode of jouissance. The same fight can therefore serve two different unconscious functions.

This is where the volume works particularly well beside Morgan's _Couple Relations_. Morgan helps identify the relational system. Busiol's contributors help disaggregate that system back into two divided subjects whose speech cannot be fully reduced to the couple pattern.

Several chapters also make an important technical point about contemporary symptoms. Panic, addiction, eating disorders, bodily enactments, identity fragility, and states of emptiness may not initially behave like the classical neurotic symptom that asks to be interpreted. The symptom may instead be doing essential work: supplying a boundary, a stable identity, a repetitive satisfaction, or protection from an overwhelming Other. Interpretation that ignores that function can therefore be premature.

The volume should not, however, be treated as a single authoritative statement of "the Lacanian clinic". It contains multiple schools and some highly specific theoretical extensions. Recalcati's clinic of the void, Calciolari's cyphermatic vocabulary, and particular contributors' social diagnoses should remain attributed to them. Primary Lacan should continue to anchor the corpus whenever a conceptual claim about Lacan himself is at stake.

The highest-value discipline to import into everyday reflection is simple but demanding:

> Preserve the exact words before deciding what they mean.

Then ask what those words do in the relation: what they demand, whom they address, what position they assign to the speaker, what uncertainty they try to close, and what recurrent satisfaction they keep in place.

That moves the Notebook away from personality typing and generic advice and toward something much closer to a Lacanian use of language: not explaining the subject from outside, but making it possible to hear where the subject is divided by what they themselves say.
