# Clinical Handbook of Couple Therapy, Fifth Edition

## Comprehensive Comparative Clinical Synthesis and Lacanian Crosswalk

### Editors
Alan S. Gurman, Jay L. Lebow, and Douglas K. Snyder

### Edition
Fifth edition, The Guilford Press, 2015

---

# 1. Executive judgment

This book is highly relevant to a Lacanian research corpus if it is used for the right purpose.

It is **not** a Lacanian text and should not be used to define Lacanian concepts. Its value is different. It is one of the strongest broad clinical maps of couple therapy available in a single volume. The handbook deliberately compares multiple schools of couple therapy using a recurring set of questions: what each approach thinks a healthy and dysfunctional couple is, how problems are maintained, what the therapist does, how assessment and goals are handled, what mechanisms are supposed to produce change, and what evidence supports the model.

For a Lacanian notebook, that makes the handbook useful in four ways.

First, it supplies a **comparative control group**. Lacanian propositions about desire, demand, fantasy, anxiety, love, jouissance, sexual non-relation, transference, symptom, and the Other can be tested against the assumptions made by mainstream couple therapies rather than treated as an isolated conceptual universe.

Second, it offers a **clinical phenomenology of couple life**. Across otherwise incompatible schools, the book repeatedly returns to recognizable phenomena: pursuit and withdrawal, criticism and defensiveness, coercive escalation, emotional disengagement, inability to tolerate difference, rigid demands for the partner to change, failures of repair, sexual anxiety, betrayal, secrets, triangles, unresolved family-of-origin patterns, difficulties with dependency and autonomy, and the interaction of individual symptoms with the couple bond.

Third, it gives a disciplined way to distinguish **description from explanation**. For example, a demand-withdraw sequence can be described behaviorally, as an attachment protest, as a systemic feedback loop, as a manifestation of object-relational projections, as a failure of differentiation, or in a Lacanian register as a recurring organization of demand, desire, fantasy, lack, and the Other. The observable sequence may be similar while the causal theory differs radically.

Fourth, it is especially valuable for practical boundary conditions. Lacanian self-analysis can become overly interpretive if it ignores mundane but decisive factors such as coercion, violence, substance misuse, severe affect dysregulation, medical illness, trauma, or simple incompatibility of life goals. The handbook repeatedly insists that these factors require direct assessment and, in some situations, take precedence over interpretive work.

The correct corpus status is therefore:

> **Secondary comparative and clinical reference. Use it to sharpen, challenge, and operationalize Lacanian inquiry, not to replace primary Lacan or to translate every clinical phenomenon into Lacanian terminology.**

# 2. Source profile and editorial logic

The fifth edition presents itself as an encyclopedic survey of the major approaches to couple therapy, with an emphasis on both conceptual breadth and empirical accountability. Rather than promoting one school, the editors place rival models into a common comparative structure. The recurring dimensions include:

- historical origins and theoretical influences;
- assumptions about healthy and dysfunctional relationships;
- structure and duration of therapy;
- therapist stance and therapeutic alliance;
- assessment and treatment planning;
- goal setting;
- characteristic techniques and process;
- proposed mechanisms of change;
- applicability, contraindications, and evidence.

This comparative architecture is one of the most useful features for a research notebook because it permits direct model-to-model contrast without pretending that the models mean the same thing by terms such as *emotion*, *attachment*, *acceptance*, *insight*, *system*, *meaning*, or *relationship*.

The handbook divides into two major parts.

2. Cognitive-Behavioral Couple Therapy

The architecture itself is clinically instructive. Part I asks, in effect, **What is a couple problem and how does change happen?** Part II asks, **What happens when a relationship problem intersects with danger, betrayal, social structure, psychiatric symptoms, sexuality, illness, or neurobiological regulation?**

# 3. How to use this source in a Lacanian notebook

Use three analytical layers and keep them distinct.

## Layer A: Source-faithful clinical reading

Ask what the handbook itself says without translating it into Lacanian vocabulary. Preserve model-specific assumptions and techniques.

## Layer B: Comparative reading

Ask how two or more handbook models describe the same phenomenon differently. Example: a partner's withdrawal may be treated as a behavior reinforced by interaction, an attachment deactivation strategy, a systemic attempt to regulate conflict, a defense against engulfment, or an object-relational reenactment.

## Layer C: Lacanian cross-reading

Only after Layers A and B, ask what becomes visible through Lacanian concepts such as:

- demand and desire;
- lack;
- fantasy;
- object *a*;
- anxiety;
- alienation and separation;
- imaginary rivalry;
- symbolic mediation;
- the big Other;
- jouissance;
- love as addressed to the Other;
- the sexual non-relation;
- symptom and sinthome;
- transference and the subject supposed to know.

The discipline is important. A useful Lacanian notebook should not collapse *attachment need* into *demand*, *projection* into *fantasy*, *differentiation* into *separation*, *communication* into *speech*, or *shared meaning* into the *Symbolic*. These can be productive comparisons, but they are not synonyms.

# 5. Chapter-by-chapter synthesis

## Chapter 1. The Theory and Practice of Couple Therapy
### Alan S. Gurman

### Core contribution

The opening chapter functions as the book's conceptual operating system. It provides a short history of couple therapy and, more importantly, establishes the common questions used to compare otherwise heterogeneous therapies.

The historical account moves from early marriage counseling, through psychoanalytic experimentation, into the family-systems revolution, and then into the contemporary period of diversification and integration. A key point for a Lacanian reader is that psychoanalytic ideas were not simply superseded. They receded during the systems revolution and later returned through object relations, psychodynamic integration, and broader pluralist approaches.

### Central comparative problem

Every couple therapy contains a theory of what is wrong, even when it claims to be purely pragmatic. Different models locate dysfunction in different places:

- observable interaction;
- distorted cognition;
- failed emotional responsiveness;
- insecure attachment;
- structural organization;
- family-of-origin transmission;
- unconscious reenactment;
- rigid narrative;
- failed problem-solving attempts;
- cultural or power arrangements.

The chapter stresses that definitions of health and pathology are never entirely neutral. Each model carries a worldview and values about intimacy, autonomy, communication, emotional expression, individuality, dependence, conflict, and change.

### Common clinical domains

Despite theoretical differences, the chapter identifies recurring goals across approaches:

- reduce severe presenting symptoms or distress;
- interrupt interaction patterns that maintain problems;
- improve communication and problem-solving capacity;
- improve conflict management;
- increase trust, intimacy, and equity where relevant;
- help each partner develop rather than requiring one to be absorbed into the couple;
- improve the couple's capacity to function in wider social systems;
- improve awareness of the reciprocal relationship between individual health and couple interaction.

It also distinguishes *ultimate goals* from *mediating or process goals*. Insight, emotion awareness, communication training, behavioral change, and pattern interruption can all be means rather than ends.

### Common factors

The chapter is important because it refuses a simplistic “which school wins?” framing. It notes evidence suggesting that several established therapies produce broadly comparable outcomes and argues for attention to common factors such as:

- motivation;
- hope;
- therapist alliance with both partners;
- coherent rationale;
- empathic attunement;
- interrupting maladaptive cycles;
- building shared responsibility for change;
- increasing mutual acceptance;
- improving communication and problem-solving;
- normalizing conflict where appropriate.

### Lacanian use

This chapter is an excellent methodological guardrail. It forces a Lacanian reader to make explicit what a Lacanian formulation assumes a couple problem *is*. It also exposes the normative question: if analysis is not simply aiming at harmony, adaptation, or “healthy communication,” what precisely counts as therapeutic movement?

The chapter can be paired with Seminar VII on ethics, Seminar VIII on transference and love, Seminar X on anxiety, and Seminar XX on love and the sexual non-relation.

## Chapter 2. Cognitive-Behavioral Couple Therapy
### Donald H. Baucom, Norman B. Epstein, Jennifer S. Kirby, and Jaslean J. LaTaillade

### Core model

Cognitive-Behavioral Couple Therapy (CBCT) integrates behavioral learning theory, cognitive therapy, and social-cognition research. The contemporary version presented here is broader than early behavioral marital therapy. It includes behavior, cognition, emotion, individual vulnerabilities, relationship themes, and environmental stressors.

### Distress formulation

Early behavioral models emphasized the ratio of rewarding to punishing exchanges and deficits in communication or problem-solving. The expanded model treats these as only part of the picture. Distress can arise from:

- differences in partners' needs and preferences;
- maladaptive strategies for pursuing those needs;
- distorted attributions about why the partner behaves as they do;
- rigid assumptions and standards about what a relationship “should” be;
- patterns of selective attention;
- expectations about the future;
- difficulty regulating emotion;
- external stressors;
- individual psychopathology.

A useful distinction is made between **primary distress** and **secondary distress**. Primary distress arises from genuine differences or unmet needs. Secondary distress arises from the hostile, coercive, avoidant, or otherwise maladaptive ways partners react to those differences. Therapy may not eliminate the primary difference, but it can reduce the secondary damage.

### Assessment logic

The model advocates structured assessment of:

- each partner individually;
- the dyadic interaction;
- the relationship's developmental history;
- family-of-origin and prior-relationship influences;
- environmental pressures;
- violence and aggression;
- substance use;
- sexuality;
- psychological symptoms;
- relationship strengths.

Direct observation of couple interaction is treated as useful because it reveals process rather than relying solely on retrospective reports.

### Key cognitive variables

The chapter distinguishes:

- selective attention;
- attributions;
- expectancies;
- assumptions;
- standards.

This is valuable for self-analysis because it gives a disciplined way to ask: *What exactly am I concluding from the partner's behavior, and on what basis?* It prevents the immediate fusion of event and meaning.

### Interventions

The approach uses:

- guided behavior change;
- communication and listening skills;
- structured decision-making;
- homework;
- Socratic questioning where safe;
- guided discovery;
- revision of rigid relationship standards;
- emotion-access work;
- strategies for affect regulation;
- relapse prevention and booster sessions.

The authors caution against reducing CBCT to mechanical communication training. A rich case formulation should connect specific techniques to the couple's actual patterns and themes.

### Lacanian comparison

CBCT and Lacanian work ask very different questions. CBCT generally assumes that cognition can be rendered more balanced, needs can be negotiated, and interactions can be made more adaptive. Lacanian analysis is more suspicious of the idea that conflict is mainly a product of inaccurate thought or skills deficits.

Still, CBCT is extremely useful descriptively. Its distinction between event, attribution, expectation, emotional response, and behavior can help isolate the sequence in which signifiers acquire personal force. A Lacanian cross-reading can then ask not only whether an attribution is accurate, but why *this* particular meaning becomes compelling, what demand it carries, what fantasy organizes it, and what satisfaction may be attached to repeating it.

## Chapter 3. Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy
### Andrew Christensen, Sona Dimidjian, and Christopher R. Martell

### Core model

Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy (IBCT) developed partly from dissatisfaction with the limitations of Traditional Behavioral Couple Therapy. The central shift is from an almost exclusive emphasis on **change** to a deliberate integration of **change and emotional acceptance**.

### Basic proposition

Differences between partners are inevitable. Distress does not arise simply because two people differ. It arises when partners respond destructively to differences they cannot readily eliminate.

The chapter identifies a characteristic progression:

1. differences become emotionally charged;
2. each partner attempts to change the other;
3. these attempts become coercive;
4. partners increasingly define difference as defect;
5. polarization intensifies;
6. partners become alienated.

The most important practical insight is that the effort to eliminate a difference can become more destructive than the difference itself.

### Four destructive processes

The model highlights:

- mutual escalating coercion;
- vilification;
- polarization;
- alienation.

Partners may move *against* the other, move *away*, cling or pursue, or recruit others into the conflict.

### Acceptance

Acceptance is not resignation. It means a reduced tendency to respond to an aversive difference by attempting to destroy, escape, or coercively alter it. In successful acceptance, partners can remain in contact with difference without treating difference as proof that the relationship or partner is defective.

### DEEP formulation

A particularly useful assessment structure is **DEEP**:

- **Differences** between partners;
- **Emotional sensitivities** arising from personal histories;
- **External circumstances** that amplify the problem;
- **Pattern of interaction** through which attempted solutions worsen it.

The DEEP formulation aims to identify a central couple theme such as closeness-distance, control-responsibility, or spontaneity-predictability.

### Major interventions

IBCT uses acceptance strategies such as:

- empathic joining around vulnerable experience;
- unified detachment, in which partners examine the pattern as a shared problem rather than an enemy located in the other;
- tolerance-building;
- contingency-shaped experiential change;
- selective use of communication and problem-solving strategies.

A key distinction is between rule-governed behavior and change that emerges through altered relational contingencies. IBCT prefers change that becomes natural and self-reinforcing rather than behavior maintained only because “the therapist said to do it.”

### Lacanian comparison

IBCT is particularly useful for a Lacanian notebook because it accepts that some differences are not soluble. That can be placed in productive tension with Lacan's stronger proposition that there is no formula that writes a complementary sexual relation.

The models should not be collapsed. IBCT still assumes that emotional acceptance can produce a more satisfying functional relationship. Lacan's non-relation concerns a structural impossibility, not merely personality differences. Even so, IBCT offers a clinically grounded question worth retaining: *How much suffering in the couple comes from the difference itself, and how much from the demand that the difference disappear?*

## Chapter 4. Emotionally Focused Couple Therapy
### Susan M. Johnson

### Core model

Emotionally Focused Couple Therapy (EFT) organizes couple distress primarily through attachment theory. The couple bond is treated as an adult attachment relationship, and emotional responses are understood as signals of attachment threat, insecurity, accessibility, responsiveness, and engagement.

### Key principles

The chapter treats emotion as a primary organizer of perception, action, and relational response. Distressed couples become caught in self-reinforcing negative cycles in which each partner's protective behavior evokes the very response they fear from the other.

Typical sequences include pursuit-withdrawal, criticism-defensiveness, clinging-distancing, or mutual attack. Surface emotions such as anger are often treated as reactive or secondary to more vulnerable experiences such as fear, loneliness, helplessness, shame, or longing.

The model assumes that attachment needs are fundamentally legitimate. Dysfunction is located not in having dependency needs but in the rigid and defensive ways those needs become enacted under perceived insecurity.

### Therapeutic movement

EFT typically proceeds through three broad stages:

1. de-escalation of the negative cycle;
2. restructuring of interaction through new emotional engagement;
3. consolidation of new interactional positions and new solutions.

The therapist helps partners identify the negative cycle as the shared enemy, access more primary emotional experience, communicate vulnerable attachment needs, and respond in ways that create greater safety.

A major change event is the “softening” in which a previously attacking or pursuing partner expresses vulnerable need in a way that invites rather than coerces response, while the previously withdrawn partner becomes more accessible and engaged.

### Therapist stance

The therapist is collaborative but highly active in process. The stance is less about adjudicating facts than tracking emotional sequence, deepening affect, reframing reactions in attachment terms, and choreographing new interaction.

### Lacanian comparison

EFT is one of the most important contrasts for Lacanian work because both approaches take seriously that ordinary content is not enough to explain intimate conflict. Yet they locate the deeper logic differently.

EFT generally interprets a demand for reassurance as an attachment signal seeking security. A Lacanian reading may ask what demand conceals about desire, why no reassurance is finally enough, what is being asked of the Other beyond the explicit request, and how the partner is recruited into fantasy.

The models diverge sharply on lack. EFT aims to create a reliably responsive bond that reduces attachment insecurity. Lacan treats lack as constitutive and not finally removable by a responsive partner. This difference should be preserved rather than harmonized.

For self-reflection, however, EFT offers a valuable descriptive move: beneath accusatory speech, identify the vulnerable affect and feared relational consequence. Then a Lacanian layer can ask what signifiers, fantasies, and demands organize that vulnerability.

## Chapter 5. Gottman Couple Therapy
### John Mordechai Gottman and Julie Schwartz Gottman

### Core model

Gottman Couple Therapy is distinctive for its heavy reliance on observational and longitudinal relationship research. It organizes therapy around conflict processes, friendship and intimacy, positive affect, physiological regulation, and shared meaning.

### Perpetual problems and gridlock

One of the most useful findings for a Lacanian reader is the claim that much couple conflict is **perpetual** rather than solvable. Enduring personality and preference differences remain. The therapeutic issue is whether these differences can be held in dialogue or become gridlocked.

Gridlock is linked to escalation into criticism, defensiveness, contempt, and stonewalling, the well-known “Four Horsemen.” A different danger is emotional disengagement, in which conflict becomes affectively empty and the relationship loses positive connection.

### Sound Relationship House

The chapter's model integrates several domains, including:

- knowing the partner's inner world through “love maps”;
- fondness and admiration;
- turning toward rather than away from bids for connection;
- maintaining a positive perspective;
- managing conflict;
- making life dreams possible;
- creating shared meaning;
- with trust and commitment treated as foundational dimensions.

The model emphasizes that good relationships do not eliminate conflict. They regulate it differently and preserve friendship, humor, affection, repair, and responsiveness alongside disagreement.

### Bids and repair

A “bid” is an attempt to establish emotional connection. Repeated turning away or turning against such bids can erode connection, while responsiveness to bids builds a reservoir of positive sentiment that makes later conflict easier to repair.

Repair attempts are especially important. A strong friendship and positive sentiment climate raise the probability that repair will be accepted rather than rejected.

### Dreams within conflict

Perpetual disagreement may become less destructive when each partner understands the deeper life dream, identity issue, value, or existential concern represented by the other's position. The goal is not necessarily to settle the disagreement but to transform the form of dialogue around it.

### Physiological regulation

The model takes flooding and physiological arousal seriously. When partners are too activated, problem-solving deteriorates. Self-soothing and de-escalation are therefore treated as prerequisites for productive engagement.

### Sexuality and intimacy

The chapter includes sexuality within the broader friendship-intimacy system. Sexual connection is linked with affection, play, romance, curiosity about the partner, and openness to erotic communication rather than treated as a detached technical subsystem.

### Lacanian comparison

Gottman is not a theory of the unconscious. Its value for Lacanian work is empirical and descriptive. It provides a grounded catalogue of what distressed couples repeatedly do.

The concept of a perpetual problem is particularly useful as a comparison, but it is not the same as Lacan's sexual non-relation. Gottman's “perpetual” usually refers to enduring differences that can be managed through dialogue. Lacan's non-relation is a structural proposition about the impossibility of a signifier or formula that would write complementary sexual relation.

Still, a useful question emerges: *Which conflicts are practical problems to solve, which are differences to negotiate, and which are recurrent sites where each partner tries to make the other answer an impossible demand?*


## Chapter 6. Integrative Problem-Centered Metaframeworks Approach
### William M. Pinsof, Douglas C. Breunlin, Anthony L. Chambers, Alexandra H. Solomon, and William P. Russell

### Core model

The Integrative Problem-Centered Metaframeworks (IPCM) approach is explicitly pluralist. Rather than treating one theoretical school as sufficient, it organizes clinical work through a set of metaframeworks that direct attention to different levels of the problem. The therapist is encouraged to use the least complex level that can produce meaningful change, while moving to deeper or more distal constraints when current-focused interventions fail.

The approach is therefore not eclectic in the loose sense of selecting techniques according to taste. It seeks an organized method for deciding **where the problem is constrained and at what level intervention is most likely to unlock change**.

### Here-and-now versus there-and-then

The chapter distinguishes more proximal metaframeworks from more historical ones. The proximal side includes:

- action and interaction;
- emotion and meaning;
- biological and behavioral factors.

The historical and intrapsychic side includes:

- family-of-origin influences;
- internal representations;
- organization of self.

The past becomes clinically relevant when it functions as a remote constraint on a present adaptive sequence. This is a useful discipline for psychodynamic work because it resists excavating history merely because history exists.

### Problem-centered integration

The model treats a presenting problem as a sequence maintained by constraints. The therapist asks what prevents a more adaptive sequence from occurring. A couple may know what to do but fail to do it because of fear, meaning, learned behavior, traumatic association, family loyalty, psychiatric symptoms, or a deeper representational pattern.

The approach therefore legitimizes interventions from multiple traditions, including cognitive-behavioral, emotionally focused, dialectical-behavioral, systemic, family-of-origin, and psychodynamic work.

### Lacanian comparison

IPCM is useful as an antidote to theoretical imperialism. It reminds the Lacanian reader that not every recurrent couple problem should be explained through fantasy, jouissance, or the Other when a concrete behavioral, contextual, or safety constraint is doing most of the work.

At the same time, the approach's internal-representation and self metaframeworks remain grounded in models of psychic organization different from Lacan's. A Lacanian notebook can borrow the **discipline of level selection** without importing the metapsychology.

A practical question to retain is:

> At what level is the current problem actually constrained, and what evidence would justify moving to a deeper interpretation?

## Chapter 7. Functional Analytic Couple Therapy
### Alan S. Gurman

### Core model

Functional Analytic Couple Therapy (FACT) is an integrative approach strongly influenced by functional contextualism and evidence-based practice. It rejects a fixed catalogue of supposedly healthy relationship behaviors and instead asks what a behavior **does** in this specific relationship and context.

The chapter criticizes earlier behavioral approaches for mistaking the topography of behavior for its function. Two couples may display the same behavior for entirely different reasons, and different behaviors may serve the same function.

### Functional analysis

A case formulation attempts to identify variables that are:

- important enough to explain a substantial portion of the problem;
- plausibly causal or at least reliably antecedent to the pattern;
- controllable through available intervention.

The chapter is appropriately cautious about causal certainty. Functional formulations are treated as provisional hypotheses that must be revised in light of what happens in therapy.

### No privileged level of explanation

FACT can include:

- overt behavior;
- thoughts and beliefs;
- affect;
- conscious or unconscious processes;
- family-system patterns;
- sociocultural factors;
- biology and physiology;
- historical learning.

This breadth is important. FACT does not insist that the couple is the only client. Individual and dyadic change are mutually related, and useful treatment may require movement at either level.

### Goal setting

Goals are case-specific. The model is comfortable with aims focused on action or reflection, attachment or differentiation, feeling or doing, bonding or bargaining. It resists defining one ideal form of intimacy.

### Clinical attitude

The therapist should remain empirically curious. A formulation is useful to the extent that it predicts, organizes, and facilitates change. This makes FACT a good comparative discipline for any interpretive therapy.

### Lacanian comparison

FACT has almost no Lacanian vocabulary, but it asks a question Lacanian self-analysis often needs: **What function is this interpretation serving now?**

A subject may produce a sophisticated explanation of childhood, desire, fantasy, or jouissance that is intellectually persuasive but functionally defensive. FACT's pragmatism encourages scrutiny of whether insight changes the relational sequence or merely becomes another element within it.

The major divergence is epistemological. FACT tends toward functional and empirically testable formulations. Lacanian analysis is concerned with effects of signification, division of the subject, and forms of enjoyment that are not reducible to observable function. These approaches can discipline each other without being merged.

## Chapter 8. Object Relations Couple Therapy
### Judith P. Siegel

### Why this chapter matters most for the Lacanian corpus

This is the handbook's most directly psychoanalytic chapter and therefore one of the most important for comparative use. It should be read carefully precisely because it is close enough to Lacan to invite false equivalence.

### Basic premise

Object relations couple therapy assumes that core aspects of self and expectations of others are formed through early relationships. The internalized “representational world” retains patterns of self-in-relation-to-object that continue to shape adult intimacy.

The chapter treats partner choice, conflict style, expression of need, vulnerability, trust, dependency, caregiving, and expectations of soothing as influenced by these internalized relational patterns.

Early experience is not treated as destiny. The central claim is that residues of early relationships continue to shape current experience until they become available for recognition, integration, and revision.

### Major theoretical influences

The chapter foregrounds contributions from:

- Otto Kernberg and Edith Jacobson, particularly splitting, idealization, devaluation, and polarized good/bad representations;
- Heinz Kohut and self psychology, especially empathy, validation, and selfobject needs;
- Margaret Mahler, separation-individuation, object constancy, and the tension between autonomy and dependency.

### Splitting

Splitting protects idealized good representations from bad or aggressive ones. In adult couple life this can appear as abrupt movement between idealization and devaluation, difficulty maintaining a complex view of the partner under emotional stress, and impulsive conclusions that the relationship or partner is entirely good or entirely bad.

The therapeutic aim is greater integration of ambivalence. A partner can be disappointing without becoming worthless; conflict need not erase love; need need not prove weakness; autonomy need not equal abandonment.

### Projective identification

Projective identification is central. One partner may disown an affect, need, vulnerability, or self-state and induce or recruit the other into carrying or enacting it. The receiving partner then appears to confirm the first partner's representation.

This produces interlocking dynamics in which each person's defensive organization helps stabilize the other's.

### Countertransference

The chapter treats therapist countertransference as a source of information about the relational field. Feelings of being judged, helpless, seduced, excluded, pressured, bored, or compelled may reproduce themes enacted by the couple. The therapist is expected to examine these reactions rather than automatically act from them.

### Treatment process

Key processes include:

- creating safety and a holding environment;
- slowing polarized states;
- identifying splitting and projection;
- tracing current reactions to earlier relational templates;
- interpreting reenactments;
- helping partners integrate complexity;
- using empathy and attunement;
- reducing unrealistic demands that the partner repair every earlier deprivation;
- cultivating more differentiated intimacy.

### Lacanian comparison

This chapter is useful, but the theoretical differences are fundamental.

Object relations theory often assumes an internal world populated by representations of self and objects derived from developmental relations. Lacan's “object” is not simply an internal representation of another person, and *object a* is emphatically not a loved or hated internal object. Lacan also resists a developmental ideal in which psychic health consists in integrating a coherent good-enough object and ego.

Likewise, projective identification is not interchangeable with fantasy, transference, or the Lacanian imaginary. A Lacanian formulation asks how the subject is positioned by signifiers and fantasy, how the partner comes to occupy the place of object *a*, and what desire or jouissance is sustained through that positioning.

The productive use is comparative:

- Object relations asks what part of self is projected into the partner.
- Lacan asks how the subject's fantasy structures what the partner is made to embody.
- Object relations asks whether ambivalent representations can be integrated.
- Lacan asks what division, lack, and impossible satisfaction the subject tries to cover through the partner.

Both can illuminate repetitive intimacy, but they are not the same explanatory system.

## Chapter 9. Bowen Family Systems Couple Coaching
### Katharine Gratwick Baker

### Core model

Bowen theory treats the family as a multigenerational emotional system. Couple functioning cannot be understood only as interaction between two autonomous individuals. Each partner enters the relationship already embedded in patterns of anxiety, attachment, cutoff, projection, sibling position, and intergenerational transmission.

### Eight concepts

The chapter reviews Bowen's central concepts:

1. differentiation of self;
2. emotional triangle;
3. nuclear family emotional system;
4. family projection process;
5. emotional cutoff;
6. multigenerational transmission process;
7. sibling position;
8. emotional process in society.

The first seven have direct relevance to couple work.

### Differentiation

Differentiation concerns the ability to define and maintain a self while remaining in emotional contact with significant others. It is not simple independence. A differentiated person can stay connected without automatically adopting the other's emotional position and can hold a principle under relational pressure.

The model contrasts the forces of individuality and togetherness. Under chronic anxiety, togetherness pressure can become intense, producing fusion, reactivity, overfunctioning-underfunctioning patterns, conflict, or cutoff.

### Mate selection

Bowen theory proposes that partners tend to select one another at roughly comparable levels of differentiation even when their surface functioning differs. One may appear highly competent and the other dependent, yet the two positions can be reciprocally organized responses to the same anxiety field.

### Triangles

The emotional triangle is treated as the smallest stable relational unit. When anxiety rises in a dyad, a third person or activity may be recruited to stabilize it. The third can be a child, parent, friend, mentor, therapist, affair partner, work, substance, ideology, or symptom.

The therapist or coach must resist being recruited into a two-against-one configuration.

### Clinical stance

Bowen preferred “coaching” to therapy. The coach aims to remain calm, factual, and detriangled while helping partners observe patterns, reduce chronic anxiety, and take responsibility for their own participation in the system.

### Lacanian comparison

Bowen offers a strong comparator for Lacanian concepts of the dyad and the third. Both traditions reject the fantasy that the intimate pair can be fully understood as a self-contained two-person unit.

But the “third” means different things. Bowen's triangle is an observable emotional-system configuration. Lacanian symbolic mediation concerns the structure of language, law, signification, and the Other. The Name-of-the-Father, for example, should never be translated into “a healthy third person in the system.”

Differentiation also differs from Lacanian separation. Bowen's differentiated self is an organismic-systemic developmental achievement. Lacanian separation concerns the subject's relation to the desire of the Other and the extraction of object *a*. Still, the comparison is fertile around questions such as:

- Can I stay in relation without making the other's affect my command?
- Whom or what do we recruit when our dyad becomes intolerable?
- Which third party is being asked to stabilize something the couple cannot symbolize directly?

## Chapter 10. Narrative Couple Therapy
### Jill Freedman and Gene Combs

### Core model

Narrative therapy treats identities and relationships as organized through stories rather than discovered as fixed inner truths. People live within narratives shaped not only by personal history but by wider cultural discourses concerning gender, marriage, sexuality, success, normality, responsibility, and power.

### Problem-saturated stories

Distress intensifies when a dominant problem story narrows what can be seen. Partners selectively notice events that confirm a rigid identity account such as “she is controlling,” “he never cares,” “we are incompatible,” or “I am the defective one.” Experiences that do not fit the story become unstoried or invisible.

### Externalization

A signature intervention is to separate the person from the problem. Rather than “you are jealous,” therapy may examine how “jealousy” operates in the relationship, what invites it, what feeds it, and when the couple has been able to resist its influence.

This reduces moral fusion between person and symptom and can open space for alternative action.

### Unique outcomes and re-authoring

Therapy searches for events that contradict the problem-saturated account, then thickens these exceptions into alternative stories about identity and relationship. New stories become more durable when they are named, elaborated, enacted, and circulated to meaningful audiences.

### Discourse and power

Drawing significantly from Michel Foucault, the chapter treats social discourses as historically situated regimes of knowledge and power. A couple's private conflict may be shaped by culturally dominant assumptions about masculinity, femininity, monogamy, parenting, normal sexuality, class, ethnicity, or what a “real” marriage should look like.

### Lacanian comparison

Narrative therapy has obvious surface resonance with Lacan because both reject a naive psychology of transparent inner essence and both take language seriously. But the similarities can mislead.

Narrative therapy is broadly social-constructionist and therapeutic change is often framed as re-authoring more preferred stories. Lacanian theory treats the signifier as structurally constitutive of the subject and emphasizes unconscious determination, division, and the Real. The aim is not simply to replace a bad narrative with a better one.

A productive cross-reading asks:

- Which dominant social signifiers organize how the couple recognizes itself?
- Which master signifiers such as “good husband,” “loyal wife,” “independent person,” “successful family,” or “normal sex life” structure conflict?
- Where does re-authoring help, and where does something persist that cannot be narratively repaired?

## Chapter 11. Solution-Focused Couple Therapy
### Michael F. Hoyt

### Core model

Solution-Focused Brief Therapy (SFBT) is constructivist, collaborative, competency-based, and future-oriented. It deliberately minimizes explanation of pathology and instead asks what the clients want to be different, when the problem is already less powerful, and how small changes can be amplified.

### Preferred future

Goals should be concrete, behaviorally observable, achievable, and phrased as the presence of desired behavior rather than merely the absence of symptoms.

The famous “miracle question” asks clients to imagine that the problem has been solved without their knowing how and then describe what would be observably different. This moves the conversation from abstract complaint to specific indicators of preferred life.

### Exceptions

The therapist searches for times when the problem could have occurred but did not, or when it was less intense. These exceptions are treated as evidence that useful capacities already exist.

### Scaling and incremental change

Clients may rate progress, confidence, motivation, or problem intensity on scales. The focus is usually not on reaching an idealized ten immediately but on identifying what would move the system one point in a preferred direction.

### Language and collaboration

The model assumes that how problems are spoken about affects the possibilities clients can perceive. It prefers language of strengths, options, exceptions, collaboration, and “not yet” over pathology, resistance, defect, and impossibility.

### Lacanian comparison

Solution-focused therapy demonstrates the performative power of speech, but it does not offer a theory of the unconscious. For a Lacanian notebook its chief use is practical and corrective. It asks whether interpretation has become excessive and whether there are already observable moments when a supposedly total problem loosens.

The Lacanian caution is equally important. An exception is not necessarily liberation from the fantasy sustaining the symptom. A preferred future may itself be organized by an unexamined ideal or by the demand of the Other. The model's optimism should therefore be used as a pragmatic tool, not a substitute for analysis of desire.

## Chapter 12. Brief Strategic Couple Therapy
### Michael J. Rohrbaugh and Varda Shoham

### Core model

This chapter presents the Palo Alto tradition associated with the Mental Research Institute. Its central concept is **ironic problem maintenance**. People respond to a problem with attempted solutions that make sense locally but recursively reproduce or intensify the problem.

### The attempted solution becomes the problem

The demand-withdraw sequence is paradigmatic. One partner presses because the other withdraws; the other withdraws because the first presses. Each treats their behavior as a rational response to the other's behavior. The attempted correction from each side becomes the stimulus for the other's counter-correction.

The model is less interested in finding the original cause than in identifying the present loop that keeps the complaint alive.

### Clinical sequence

The brief strategic template includes:

- define the complaint behaviorally and concretely;
- establish modest goals;
- identify what the clients have repeatedly tried;
- map the ironic loop in which “more of the same” maintains the problem;
- interrupt the loop through a strategically chosen intervention.

### Strategic interventions

Interventions may include:

- doing less of the attempted solution;
- doing its opposite;
- reframing the partner's behavior;
- “go slow” messages;
- therapeutic restraint;
- paradoxical or counterintuitive tasks;
- instructions that reveal the structure of the problem by exaggerating or prescribing it.

The therapist uses the clients' own language and worldview to make interventions acceptable and persuasive.

### Lacanian comparison

The model's account of ironic repetition is highly useful for self-analysis. It encourages the question:

> What do I repeatedly do to solve this problem that may actually be reproducing it?

A Lacanian layer can then ask why the “failed” solution remains compelling and what satisfaction or jouissance is attached to repetition. Strategic therapy itself generally does not require such an explanation. It is satisfied if interrupting the loop changes the problem.

This difference is productive. Lacanian analysis can explain why an unhelpful loop has subjective necessity; strategic therapy can expose when explanation itself has become another attempted solution that leaves the loop intact.

## Chapter 13. Structural Couple Therapy
### George M. Simon

### Core model

Structural Couple Therapy (SCT) derives from Salvador Minuchin's structural family therapy. The theory shifts attention from intrapsychic linear causality to circular interaction and to the organization of family and couple subsystems.

### Structure, subsystems, and boundaries

A family consists of overlapping subsystems organized by roles, generations, and functions. Boundaries regulate exchange between subsystems.

- **Diffuse boundaries** produce enmeshment, insufficient differentiation, and overinvolvement.
- **Rigid boundaries** produce disengagement and prevent resources in one subsystem from becoming available to another.
- More adaptive boundaries allow both distinction and exchange.

Within the couple, dysfunction may involve either avoidance of difference or rigidification of difference into polarized positions.

### Enactment

The signature method is **enactment**. Rather than merely describing a conflict to the therapist, partners interact with one another in the room. The therapist observes and intervenes in the live relational structure.

Enactment is not treated merely as one technique. It is the primary medium through which assessment and change occur. New relational experiences in session are intended to alter how each partner experiences the other.

### Structural assessment

The therapist observes:

- how the couple handles difference;
- external boundaries around the couple;
- alliances and coalitions;
- interaction with children and extended family;
- rigidity and permeability of relational rules;
- how the couple recruits the therapist into its existing structure.

### Cultural caution

The chapter recognizes the danger of imposing culturally specific judgments about what counts as excessive closeness, autonomy, enmeshment, or disengagement. The couple's own experience of dysfunction matters, and structural concepts should not become disguised cultural colonialism.

### Lacanian comparison

Structural therapy and Lacanian theory both challenge individualistic causality, but their meanings of “structure” are entirely different. Structural family therapy concerns observable organization of relational systems. Lacanian structuralism concerns the signifier, symbolic relations, subject positions, and the effects of language.

Even so, structural enactment is useful in a Lacanian notebook because it highlights that the couple's organization is not only *reported* in speech but can be *performed* in the interaction itself. One can ask:

- Who speaks for whom?
- Who is positioned as knowing, desiring, judging, or lacking?
- Which difference is tolerated and which must be erased?
- When a third enters, what structural function does that third acquire?

# 6. Comparative synthesis of Part I

Across the twelve principal treatment models, several recurring fault lines become visible.

## 6.1 Change versus acceptance

Behavioral approaches historically emphasized changing problematic behavior. Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy adds acceptance. Emotionally Focused Couple Therapy seeks transformation of attachment interaction rather than mere behavioral compliance. Gottman combines skill, regulation, friendship, and acceptance of perpetual issues. Object relations aims at integration of previously split or projected experience. Bowen aims at more differentiated functioning rather than changing the partner.

For Lacanian work, the key distinction is between **change of the other** and **change in one's relation to what cannot be made to disappear**.

## 6.2 Present interaction versus historical determination

Strategic, structural, solution-focused, and behavioral models often privilege current interaction. Object relations and Bowen emphasize history and family-of-origin transmission. Integrative approaches treat history as relevant when it constrains present functioning.

A Lacanian approach complicates this distinction because the past is not simply a causal archive. It persists through signifiers, repetition, fantasy, and retroactive meaning.

## 6.3 Individual versus couple as unit

Some approaches treat the couple as the main unit. Others insist that both individual organization and dyadic process matter. This is especially clear in FACT, CBCT, object relations, and the clinical chapters on psychiatric or medical problems.

For Lacan, the subject is neither an isolated individual nor a component of a dyadic organism. The subject is divided and constituted in relation to the Other. This makes the mainstream “individual versus system” debate useful but insufficient.

## 6.4 Skills deficit versus organized impossibility

Several chapters explicitly criticize the simplistic idea that distressed couples merely lack communication skills. Couples may possess the skills but fail to use them when emotionally activated. They may also use sophisticated communication in the service of coercion, reassurance seeking, accusation, or avoidance.

This is a point of strong relevance for Lacanian analysis. Knowing how one *should* communicate does not necessarily alter what one wants from the interaction or the jouissance attached to the conflict.

## 6.5 Conflict as solvable versus conflict as constitutive

The handbook ranges from problem-solving models to approaches that normalize enduring difference. Gottman's perpetual problems and IBCT's irreconcilable differences move closest to recognizing that not every conflict has a solution.

A Lacanian framework radicalizes the question: some impasses may not be accidental obstacles to an otherwise harmonious complementarity. They may be local expressions of a more constitutive non-complementarity.


# 7. Part II: Applications, populations, problems, and issues

Part II is especially important for a self-analysis or couple-therapy notebook because it prevents “the relationship” from being treated as a single homogeneous object. The same interactional pattern can mean very different things when it occurs in the context of violence, betrayal, trauma, substance dependence, severe depression, chronic illness, stepfamily structure, minority stress, or intercultural difference.

The practical lesson is simple: **before interpreting a pattern, identify the context that gives the pattern its stakes.**

## Chapter 14. Couple Therapy for Partner Aggression
### Norman B. Epstein, Carol A. Werlinich, and Jaslean J. LaTaillade

### Why this chapter is a boundary condition

This chapter is clinically important because it shows where ordinary couple-therapy assumptions become unsafe. It differentiates severe coercive violence or battering from milder, often mutual forms of psychological and physical aggression. The distinction determines whether conjoint couple therapy is appropriate at all.

### Assessment first

The authors argue that aggression is often missed when therapists rely on spontaneous disclosure. They recommend structured, multimethod assessment, including individual interviews where each partner can speak without the other present.

Assessment should consider:

- severity and frequency of aggression;
- injury;
- fear of the partner;
- coercive control;
- retaliation risk;
- substance misuse;
- sequence of events around aggressive episodes;
- psychological aggression as well as physical acts;
- whether both partners can safely participate in conjoint work.

### Contraindications

Severe violence, battering, significant fear, active danger, or untreated conditions that substantially impair safe participation are treated as reasons not to proceed with ordinary conjoint therapy.

### Treatment focus when conjoint work is appropriate

For selected couples with psychological aggression or mild to moderate physical aggression and without severe coercive control, structured treatment targets:

- anger cues and self-regulation;
- time-outs and de-escalation;
- beliefs that justify aggression;
- hostile attributions;
- communication and problem-solving deficits;
- chronic stress;
- accountability for one's own aggressive acts;
- protective factors and relationship strengths.

### Lacanian use

This chapter is a strong warning against romanticizing conflict as desire, jouissance, or “the couple's symptom” when there is coercion or danger. Psychoanalytic interpretation does not replace safety assessment.

For self-analysis, a necessary prior question is:

> Is this a symbolic conflict I am trying to understand, or is there intimidation, coercion, fear, or actual danger that requires a different response?

No Lacanian sophistication should be used to redescribe violence as a mutually produced fantasy in a way that obscures responsibility or risk.

## Chapter 15. Couple Therapy and the Treatment of Affairs
### Kristina Coop Gordon, Alexander Khaddouma, Donald H. Baucom, and Douglas K. Snyder

### Core formulation

The chapter conceptualizes an affair as a form of interpersonal betrayal that can have trauma-like effects because it violates fundamental assumptions about the partner, the relationship, and one's own judgment.

The approach integrates cognitive-behavioral work, trauma-informed principles, developmental exploration, and research on forgiveness.

### Three stages

#### Stage 1: Dealing with impact

Immediate goals are stabilization and damage control. Typical tasks include:

- assessment;
- boundary setting;
- reducing destructive escalation;
- affect regulation and self-care;
- allowing the injured partner to articulate impact;
- helping the participating partner respond without reflexive defensiveness where possible;
- managing intrusive thoughts and flashbacks;
- addressing parenting, work, and daily functioning that may have been destabilized.

#### Stage 2: Finding meaning

The couple explores the conditions surrounding the affair. The chapter resists a simplistic single-cause explanation. Contributing factors may include:

- features of the relationship;
- personal history;
- current stressors;
- family-of-origin meanings;
- opportunity and pursuit by another person;
- vulnerabilities of the participating partner;
- vulnerabilities or patterns in the couple context.

Understanding is not equated with excusing. The purpose is to construct a more adequate account of how the event became possible.

#### Stage 3: Moving on

The couple consolidates its understanding, considers forgiveness or other forms of letting go, and decides how to proceed. Continuing the relationship is not predetermined as the correct outcome.

### Forgiveness

Forgiveness is treated less as moral absolution than as a possible reduction in the need to punish, a more balanced view of the offending partner, and potentially greater compassion. It cannot be demanded as proof of recovery.

### Lacanian comparison

Affairs are fertile sites for Lacanian interpretation because they expose desire, secrecy, the gaze, the rival, fantasy, and the impossibility of making oneself the complete object of the other's desire. But the trauma framework contributes something Lacanian abstraction can miss: betrayal can shatter concrete assumptions, destabilize identity, and produce severe dysregulation.

A productive Lacanian supplement asks:

- What did exclusivity mean to each partner before the affair?
- What fantasy of being “the one” was shattered?
- What was demanded from the partner as guarantee?
- What did the third person allow the participating partner to desire, stage, escape, or become?
- How does interrogation after discovery attempt to restore a knowledge that can never be complete?
- Is “knowing everything” functioning as a fantasy of abolishing uncertainty?

These questions should supplement, not replace, the chapter's emphasis on accountability, stabilization, and concrete decision-making.

## Chapter 16. Separation and Divorce Issues in Couple Therapy
### Jay L. Lebow

### Core problem

Couple therapy does not always end in relationship preservation. Some clients enter therapy ambivalent, some use therapy to decide whether to stay, and others decide during treatment that separation is the preferable outcome.

The chapter therefore distinguishes therapy aimed at improving the relationship from therapy that assists a couple in ending it with less unnecessary damage.

### Mixed agendas

A common difficulty is asymmetry of commitment. One partner may be trying to save the relationship while the other is considering leaving. Therapy must not silently convert preservation of the couple into the therapist's own goal.

The clinician needs to clarify:

- each partner's current level of commitment;
- whether there is genuine willingness to test change;
- whether therapy is being used to postpone a decision;
- what would constitute enough evidence for each person to stay or leave;
- the implications for children and coparenting;
- practical and legal realities that may require outside professional expertise.

### Functional versus destructive divorce processes

The chapter treats divorce as a major life transition with substantial short-term disruption, while resisting the assumption that separation necessarily produces permanent dysfunction. A key clinical goal is reducing destructive conflict, especially when children remain linked to both parents.

### Lacanian comparison

This chapter is useful because it separates analytic value from relationship preservation. A Lacanian ethic cannot simply assume that staying together is the good and separation the failure.

The relevant questions become:

- What desire is sustained by staying?
- What desire is evaded by leaving?
- Is the relationship being preserved to avoid loss, guilt, status change, solitude, or confrontation with one's own desire?
- Is separation being demanded as a fantasy of finally escaping one's own repetitive position?
- What part of the couple's conflict will likely travel with each subject into the next relationship?

The chapter's practical realism keeps these questions from becoming moral verdicts.

## Chapter 17. Therapy with Couples in Stepfamilies
### Patricia L. Papernow

### Core model

Stepfamilies are not first-time families with extra members. They have a different architecture. The new couple forms in the presence of pre-existing parent-child bonds, former partners, loyalties, losses, and already established family cultures.

### Five structural challenges

The chapter identifies five recurring challenges:

1. stepparents occupy outsider positions while biological parents occupy insider positions;
2. children face loss, change, and loyalty binds;
3. parenting frequently polarizes the adults;
4. the new household must develop a shared culture out of previously established cultures;
5. former spouses, whether present, absent, or deceased, remain structurally relevant.

### Clinical levels

Intervention may occur at three levels:

- psychoeducation about normal stepfamily processes;
- interpersonal work on the couple's communication and polarization;
- intrapsychic and family-of-origin work when the structure activates older injuries.

The chapter is particularly careful about unrealistic expectations that a new stepfamily should quickly reproduce the emotional texture of a first-time family.

### Lacanian comparison

The stepfamily material is useful for thinking about the persistence of symbolic positions that cannot be erased by goodwill. A new partner does not simply replace an absent spouse or become a parent because the household would function more smoothly if everyone behaved “as one family.”

Lacanian questions include:

- Which kinship signifiers are being contested?
- Who is authorized to occupy the position of parent, spouse, outsider, rival, or child?
- Which losses are being denied by the demand to become one harmonious family?
- How do loyalty and prohibition organize desire across the new structure?

This chapter strongly supports the broader Lacanian warning against imagining the couple as an isolated dyad.

## Chapter 18. Gay, Lesbian, and Bisexual Issues in Couple Therapy
### Robert-Jay Green and Valory Mitchell

### Core contribution

The chapter questions the tendency to treat heterosexual couples as an invisible norm and lesbian, gay, and bisexual couples as a special deviation from that norm. It asks the reader to reverse the gaze and recognize heterosexuality itself as socially structured by law, norms, privileges, role expectations, and institutional support.

### Minority stress

Same-sex and bisexual couples may face stressors that arise not from their relationship dynamics but from:

- stigma;
- discrimination;
- rejection;
- invisibility;
- differential legal recognition;
- internalized negative social messages;
- ambiguity regarding norms and roles;
- uneven family or community support.

A competent formulation must not misread social stress as evidence that the couple form itself is dysfunctional.

### Within-group diversity

The chapter resists treating lesbian, gay, or bisexual couples as homogeneous. Relationship structure varies with gender, class, ethnicity, race, generation, disclosure status, family support, and many other variables.

### Lacanian comparison

For a Lacanian corpus, this chapter is important as a check against universalizing historically specific heterosexual social arrangements. Lacanian sexuation is not a sociological typology of men and women, and it should not be used to assign subjects to social roles based on anatomy, gender identity, or partner choice.

The chapter can therefore be used to keep two levels distinct:

- social-symbolic regulation of identities and relationships;
- Lacanian logical positions regarding sexuation, jouissance, and the non-relation.

The former materially shapes the conditions in which the latter are lived, but they should not be collapsed.

## Chapter 19. Couple Therapy and Intercultural Relationships
### Kyle D. Killian

### Core model

Intercultural couples encounter the same universal problem of difference as all couples, but cultural differences may be especially visible, socially marked, or politically charged. The chapter cautions against assuming that cultural difference is either automatically the problem or irrelevant to it.

### Push and pull of culture

Culture can operate as both belonging and constraint. A partner may experience family tradition, religion, ethnicity, or national identity as a source of continuity and support while also experiencing demands, prohibitions, or roles as restrictive.

The couple therefore has to negotiate not only “your culture versus mine” but each person's ambivalent relation to their own cultural inheritance.

### Domains of difference

The chapter highlights areas such as:

- emotional expressiveness;
- closeness to family of origin;
- individualism versus collectivism;
- gender roles;
- religion;
- worldview;
- orientation to past, present, and future;
- class and power;
- racism and discrimination.

### Clinical stance

The therapist needs epistemic humility. Rather than deciding in advance that cultural difference explains the problem, the clinician asks how the couple itself organizes, magnifies, minimizes, masks, or metaphorizes difference.

### Lacanian comparison

This chapter is highly relevant to the Lacanian notion that desire is mediated by the Other. The cultural Other supplies signifiers, ideals, prohibitions, and inherited narratives through which a partner becomes desirable, unacceptable, threatening, admirable, shameful, or exotic.

Useful questions include:

- Which family or cultural signifiers are spoken through each partner during conflict?
- Which ideals of husband, wife, parent, masculinity, femininity, duty, or autonomy are being enforced?
- Is “culture” naming a real difference, or is it functioning as a screen for another conflict?
- What does transgression of family expectations make possible erotically or symbolically?

## Chapter 20. Couple Therapy and Posttraumatic Stress Disorder
### Candice M. Monson and Steffany J. Fredman

### Core model

The chapter presents Cognitive-Behavioral Conjoint Therapy for Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD), a disorder-specific treatment that targets both trauma symptoms and relationship functioning.

The rationale is relational: PTSD affects intimacy, trust, avoidance, anger, sleep, sexuality, daily routines, and partner burden, while relationship patterns can reinforce avoidance or support recovery.

### Couple as treatment context

The partner can become involved in processes that maintain symptoms, often unintentionally. Examples include excessive accommodation of avoidance, reorganizing life around the traumatized partner's fear, or conflict patterns that increase arousal and threat perception.

Treatment therefore aims to:

- establish safety;
- increase understanding of PTSD;
- reduce avoidance;
- improve communication;
- examine trauma-related beliefs;
- increase behavioral approach to previously avoided situations;
- build intimacy and shared functioning.

### Lacanian comparison

Trauma has a complex place in psychoanalysis. The clinical value of this chapter is that it does not assume every posttraumatic symptom is best approached through free associative interpretation alone. Avoidance, hyperarousal, accommodation, and concrete relational consequences can require direct intervention.

A Lacanian supplement may ask how the traumatic event becomes inscribed or fails to be symbolized, how it reorganizes the subject's relation to the Other, and where repetitive intrusion marks a limit of meaning. But this should not displace evidence-based trauma care where indicated.

## Chapter 21. Couple Therapy and Alcohol Problems
### Barbara S. McCrady and Elizabeth E. Epstein

### Core model

Alcohol Behavioral Couple Therapy treats problematic drinking and the relationship as reciprocally influential. The intimate partner is not merely an observer of an individual disorder. Couple interaction can cue, reinforce, buffer, or help alter alcohol use.

### Assessment

The model assesses:

- severity and pattern of alcohol use;
- motivation and drinking goals;
- antecedents and consequences of drinking;
- partner responses;
- relationship satisfaction;
- communication;
- conflict;
- coping resources;
- relapse risk.

### Treatment

Interventions can include:

- support for abstinence or agreed drinking goals where clinically appropriate;
- behavioral contracts;
- alternative coping strategies;
- reinforcement of non-drinking behavior;
- communication and problem-solving skills;
- reducing behaviors that inadvertently cue or maintain drinking;
- relapse-prevention planning;
- rebuilding positive couple activities.

The therapist's alliance is used to support change, but transference interpretation is not treated as a central mechanism.

### Lacanian comparison

Substance use can readily invite psychoanalytic interpretation as jouissance, self-medication, oral satisfaction, avoidance of castration, or response to the Other. Those formulations may sometimes be illuminating, but this chapter provides a necessary corrective: alcohol use has concrete behavioral contingencies, physiological effects, relapse patterns, and safety implications.

A good Lacanian formulation should therefore be able to coexist with direct questions about use frequency, triggers, consequences, withdrawal, impairment, and treatment needs.

## Chapter 22. Couple Therapy and Depression
### Mark A. Whisman and Steven R. H. Beach

### Core problem

Depression and relationship distress are reciprocally related. Depression can reduce positive interaction, sexual interest, energy, problem-solving capacity, and emotional availability. Relationship conflict can also precipitate, maintain, or intensify depressive symptoms.

### Important relational processes

The chapter identifies patterns often associated with depression in couples:

- increased blame and withdrawal;
- reduced positive communication;
- loss of shared rewarding activities;
- diminished emotional disclosure;
- less effective problem-solving;
- reduced perceived support;
- criticism and denigration;
- disruption of routines and physical affection.

### Treatment selection

The temporal relation matters. If relationship distress appears central to the depressive episode, couple-based intervention may be particularly relevant. If severe individual psychopathology makes dyadic work unsafe or impossible, individual or medical treatment may need priority.

Active suicidality, psychosis, or poorly controlled severe mood disturbance are not treated as ordinary couple issues.

### Lacanian comparison

For Lacanian self-analysis, the chapter helps distinguish two questions that are easily confused:

1. What subjective logic does a depressive position have?
2. How is the couple interaction concretely maintaining or worsening depressive functioning?

A subject can learn much from interpreting loss, guilt, demand, ideal, and desire, while still needing direct treatment for a depressive disorder. One level does not invalidate the other.

## Chapter 23. Couple Therapy and Borderline Personality Disorder
### Alan E. Fruzzetti and Luciana Payne

### Core model

This chapter applies principles from Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) to couples affected by Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD) or by severe emotion dysregulation that produces similar relational patterns.

The authors note that even couples without a diagnosed partner can temporarily develop highly reactive “borderline-like” interaction in a severely distressed relationship.

### Transactional model

The key formulation is reciprocal. High emotional sensitivity and reactivity interact with invalidating responses from the partner. Each partner's attempt to manage the other's intense reaction can further amplify dysregulation.

Common patterns include:

- rapid escalation;
- fear of abandonment;
- impulsive behavior;
- invalidation;
- mutually destructive interaction;
- demand-withdraw or engage-distance cycles;
- self-harm or suicidality in more severe presentations;
- difficulty maintaining a stable sense of self under relational pressure.

### Treatment principles

DBT-informed couple work emphasizes:

- validation;
- emotion regulation;
- distress tolerance;
- mindfulness;
- behavioral analysis;
- clear treatment targets;
- reduction of invalidating transactions;
- more effective problem-solving and communication.

### Lacanian comparison

The chapter is useful because Lacanian clinicians have their own structural concept of borderline presentations only in some post-Lacanian traditions, not as a simple equivalent of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders category.

For research purposes, keep diagnostic BPD, severe affect dysregulation, and Lacanian structural diagnosis separate.

A Lacanian interpretation of abandonment, demand, splitting, or unstable identification can be valuable, but acute self-harm risk and severe dysregulation require direct clinical management. Self-analysis is not an adequate substitute.

## Chapter 24. Couple Therapy and Sexual Problems
### Gerald R. Weeks and Nancy Gambescia

### Why this chapter is especially relevant

Sexuality is one of the most directly useful points of contact between the handbook and a Lacanian corpus. The chapter uses an intersystem approach that treats sexual difficulties as potentially involving biological, behavioral, cognitive, emotional, relational, developmental, and cultural factors.

### Multicausality

Sexual difficulties may involve:

- performance anxiety;
- response anxiety;
- desire discrepancy;
- negative sexual beliefs;
- resentment;
- lack of communication;
- medical or medication factors;
- family-of-origin meanings;
- shame;
- fear of vulnerability;
- fear of anger, rejection, abandonment, dependency, or exposure;
- entrenched couple conflict;
- patterns of avoidance.

### Anxiety and desire

A particularly useful observation is that attempts to force desire can decrease it. If partners believe they *should* feel more desire and begin monitoring whether desire is sufficiently present, the monitoring itself can intensify anxiety and inhibit erotic responsiveness.

This is clinically important for a Lacanian notebook because it shows how demand addressed to desire can produce the opposite of its intended result.

### Communication

The chapter emphasizes direct communication about sexual wishes, fears, pleasure, and dissatisfaction. It notes that couples who communicate competently in other domains may still be unable to speak about sex.

### Flexible treatment

Intervention may include:

- psychoeducation;
- cognitive restructuring;
- affective exploration;
- sensate focus or graded touch exercises;
- relaxation;
- individual work followed by conjoint work;
- attention to unconscious intimacy fears;
- couple communication;
- medical assessment where relevant.

### Lacanian comparison

The central divergence is profound. Sex therapy often aims at improved function, reduced anxiety, greater pleasure, communication, and a more satisfactory sexual relationship. Lacan's theory does not begin from a model of a naturally harmonious sexual relation that has become dysfunctional.

Seminar XX's proposition that “there is no sexual relationship” should not be misread as a claim that satisfying sex is impossible. It means that no symbolic formula writes a complementary relation between sexed positions.

This chapter is therefore ideal for two-layer analysis:

**Clinical layer:** What biological, behavioral, cognitive, emotional, and relational factors are interfering with sexual functioning?

**Lacanian layer:** What does sex mean for each subject? What demand is placed on desire? What fantasy makes the partner erotically legible? What relation to jouissance is sought or defended against? What happens when love, desire, and jouissance do not line up?

The two layers should remain distinct.

## Chapter 25. Couple Therapy and Medical Issues
### Nancy Breen Ruddy and Susan H. McDaniel

### Core model

The chapter uses a biopsychosocial and family-systems perspective to examine how illness reorganizes couple life. Serious or chronic illness affects roles, dependency, sexuality, finances, parenting, identity, future expectations, caregiving, mortality awareness, and the distribution of emotional labor.

### Adaptation

The core marker of healthy functioning is not absence of distress but the couple's capacity to adapt to the demands of illness without becoming rigidly organized around it.

The therapist examines:

- illness history and prognosis;
- each partner's understanding of the illness;
- family narratives about illness;
- earlier experiences with medicine, disability, or loss;
- caregiving expectations;
- practical role changes;
- anticipated future losses;
- acceptance or denial;
- relationship with health professionals;
- socioeconomic and community resources.

A timeline can help integrate medical events with couple and family transitions.

### Collaboration with health care

The chapter emphasizes interdisciplinary work. Couple therapists may need medical information to understand symptoms, treatment effects, prognosis, or sexual and cognitive consequences of illness.

### Lacanian comparison

Illness sharply exposes the Real limits of bodily control and the inadequacy of fantasies of self-sufficiency. It can reactivate dependency, castration anxiety, fear of loss, guilt, resentment, and altered desirability.

But medical facts remain medical facts. A bodily symptom should not be psychologized merely because it has relational meaning. Lacanian interpretation is most useful in examining how the illness is signified and lived, not in replacing medical causation.

## Chapter 26. Couple Therapy and Interpersonal Neurobiology
### Mona DeKoven Fishbane

### Core contribution

This chapter does not offer a separate school of couple therapy. It attempts to translate selected findings from neuroscience and interpersonal neurobiology into clinically useful concepts about emotion regulation, empathy, habit, reactivity, and neuroplasticity.

### Reactivity

Distressed couples can become trapped in automatic cycles in which rapid threat responses dominate reflective choice. The chapter uses brain-based language to explain how heightened arousal can reduce access to more reflective regulatory capacities.

### Neuroeducation

Brief explanations of brain function may help clients depersonalize automatic reactions without evading responsibility. The point is not “my brain made me do it,” but “my reaction has an automatic component that I can learn to recognize and regulate.”

### Empathy and resonance

The chapter links empathy with embodied interpersonal processes and emphasizes the possibility of developing greater reflective and relational capacity through repeated experience.

### Neuroplasticity

Habit is understood as biologically entrenched but modifiable. Repeated new patterns can gradually become more available, providing a biological rationale for sustained practice rather than one-off insight.

### Lacanian comparison

This chapter should be used cautiously. Neuroscience and Lacanian psychoanalysis operate at different explanatory levels. Neural correlates do not settle questions about desire, meaning, fantasy, or subjectivity.

Its best use is modest: it explains why knowing something intellectually may not immediately change an automatic relational response. A Lacanian insight may be genuine while the subject still needs repeated practice before a different embodied response becomes accessible.

# 8. Cross-cutting clinical concepts across the handbook

## 8.1 The negative interaction cycle

Across incompatible schools, distress is repeatedly described as circular rather than linear.

Examples include:

- pursue → withdraw → intensified pursuit → deeper withdrawal;
- criticize → defend → escalate → contempt or shutdown;
- fear abandonment → demand reassurance → partner feels controlled → partner distances → abandonment fear increases;
- demand change → partner resists → demand intensifies → resistance hardens;
- partner drinks → other monitors and criticizes → shame and conflict increase → drinking becomes more attractive;
- trauma avoidance → partner accommodates → short-term relief → long-term avoidance strengthens.

The causal theories differ, but the circular pattern is remarkably stable across models.

### Lacanian use

Instead of asking only “who started it?”, ask:

1. What does each act mean to each partner?
2. What response is each trying to force from the other?
3. What does each response confirm?
4. What enjoyment or certainty is obtained by the repetition?
5. Which signifier organizes the cycle: abandonment, disrespect, control, betrayal, failure, inadequacy, engulfment, rejection?

---

## 8.2 Pursuit and withdrawal

Pursuit-withdrawal appears in behavioral, attachment, Gottman, Bowen, strategic, and other models.

The pursuer often experiences withdrawal as evidence of indifference, abandonment, or refusal. The withdrawer often experiences pursuit as criticism, control, engulfment, or proof that nothing they do will be enough.

Each position can therefore be experienced as defensive rather than aggressive from inside the subject's own logic.

### Lacanian use

The pursuer's explicit demand may conceal the impossible demand for proof of desire: *Show me that I matter in a way that leaves no room for doubt.*

The withdrawer may attempt to preserve a space not colonized by the other's demand, while the withdrawal itself becomes a message that intensifies the demand.

A useful question is not simply “who needs more closeness?” but:

> What would it mean if the partner responded exactly as demanded, and why would that response still perhaps fail to settle the question?

---

## 8.3 Acceptance versus the demand to change the partner

Several models converge on the view that attempts to reform the partner can become a major generator of distress. This is explicit in Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy and appears indirectly in Gottman, Bowen, and Emotionally Focused Couple Therapy.

Acceptance does not imply accepting abuse, betrayal, coercion, or violations of agreed boundaries. It concerns the capacity to live with some enduring difference without converting every difference into evidence of defect.

### Lacanian use

The demand that the partner change often carries a fantasy of what would finally make the relationship complete. This is where Lacan adds something distinctive: the partner may be asked to repair a lack that no partner can remove.

The practical question becomes:

> Which requested changes are concrete and negotiable, and which are being asked to eliminate structural uncertainty, lack, mortality, difference, or the fact that the Other has a desire of their own?

---

## 8.4 Idealization, devaluation, and polarized perception

Object relations gives this process its clearest theoretical treatment, but the phenomenon appears elsewhere as negative sentiment override, vilification, rigid attribution, or problem-saturated narrative.

Under distress, the partner can become reduced to one trait: selfish, cold, controlling, needy, unreliable, irrational, weak, sexually rejecting, or impossible to satisfy.

### Lacanian use

A Lacanian formulation asks how the partner has been made to occupy a place in fantasy. The question is not only whether the negative judgment is fair, but what subjective consistency it provides.

When the partner is reduced to “the one who always rejects me,” the subject gains an answer to the more disturbing question of why desire is uncertain.

---

## 8.5 Repair

Gottman gives repair a central empirical role, but the idea appears across approaches.

Repair may involve:

- apology;
- de-escalation;
- humor;
- accepting influence;
- acknowledging impact;
- clarification;
- a time-out followed by return;
- re-framing the cycle;
- emotional responsiveness;
- behavioral restitution;
- forgiveness after a major injury.

### Lacanian use

Repair is not the same as closure. Some injuries can be acknowledged and lived with without becoming fully “resolved.” A Lacanian perspective is particularly useful where the demand for complete repair becomes another impossible demand for the past to have been otherwise.

---

## 8.6 Secrets and the third

Affairs, hidden money, substance use, family coalitions, private alliances, children recruited into conflict, and therapist triangulation all show that the couple is rarely only two.

### Lacanian use

The third can function differently:

- as rival;
- as witness;
- as guarantor;
- as object of desire;
- as authority;
- as imagined judge;
- as escape route;
- as stabilizer of an intolerable dyad.

A third person is not automatically pathological. The more important question is what structural function the third performs.

---

## 8.7 Sexuality as a couple process

Across the sex-therapy, Gottman, medical, trauma, and illness chapters, sexual functioning is embedded in broader relational life. Desire is affected by resentment, anxiety, illness, medication, shame, body image, partner response, role overload, trust, and communication.

### Lacanian use

This reinforces a distinction fundamental to Lacan:

- love;
- desire;
- sexual satisfaction;
- attachment;
- identification;
- jouissance

are related but not identical. A loving couple can have little desire. Intense desire can coexist with poor compatibility. Good sexual function does not guarantee secure attachment. Emotional safety can increase desire for some people and reduce erotic tension for others.

---

## 8.8 The therapist as third

Every model recognizes, in different language, that the therapist can be pulled into the couple's organization.

Examples include:

- each partner recruiting the therapist as judge;
- a secret creating alliance with one partner;
- the therapist becoming rescuer, critic, parent, expert, or witness;
- countertransference reproducing a couple role;
- triangulation in Bowen's sense;
- loss of balanced alliance.

### Lacanian use

This is directly relevant to transference. The couple may position the therapist as the one supposed to know who is right, what love is, whether the marriage should continue, what the partner really means, or what a normal sexual relationship should be.

A Lacanian stance should be especially cautious about occupying that position of guarantor.


# 9. Lacanian crosswalk: concepts that should be compared but not conflated

This section is not part of the handbook itself. It is a comparative layer for use with a Lacanian corpus. The purpose is to make translation errors explicit.

---

## 9.1 “Relationship” versus Lacan's non-relation

Most couple therapies presuppose that there is a relationship entity whose functioning can become healthier or more dysfunctional. Lacan's formulation that “there is no sexual relationship” does not deny empirical relationships, marriages, attachments, households, or sexual partnerships. It denies that there is a signifying formula that writes a complementary relation between sexed beings.

Therefore:

- “healthy relationship” in the handbook is a clinical and normative construct;
- “sexual non-relation” in Lacan is a structural proposition;
- one does not refute the other;
- successful couple therapy does not abolish non-relation;
- non-relation does not imply that practical couple work is meaningless.

A useful Notebook query is:

> Which problems in this case are contingent and modifiable, and which demands appear to arise from the fantasy that the other person could provide a complete relation?

---

## 9.2 Need, attachment, demand, and desire

Emotionally Focused Couple Therapy and several other models speak in terms of needs for safety, closeness, responsiveness, support, and attachment.

Lacan distinguishes need, demand, and desire.

A biological or practical need becomes articulated through language as demand. But demand is never simply the request for an object. It is also addressed to the Other and acquires a dimension of love and recognition. Desire emerges in the gap that remains because no response to demand can fully saturate what has been asked.

This distinction is highly useful in couple conflict.

Example:

- Manifest request: “Text me if you're going to be late.”
- Practical need: predictability and coordination.
- Demand: “Show that you take me into account.”
- Possible deeper demand: “Prove I matter to you.”
- Desire: may persist precisely because no quantity of texting can finally prove one's unique place in the Other's desire.

The clinical mistake would be to call every request an impossible demand. Many requests are reasonable and negotiable. The Lacanian question becomes relevant when satisfaction of the explicit request repeatedly fails to settle the conflict.

---

## 9.3 Attachment security and the lack in the Other

Attachment-based therapies seek greater confidence that the partner will be accessible and responsive under conditions of vulnerability.

Lacan's barred Other, by contrast, means that there is no complete, self-consistent Other capable of supplying a final guarantee of meaning, identity, or desire.

A partner can become more reliable. This matters enormously. But reliability cannot guarantee:

- that love will never change;
- that desire will never shift;
- that death or separation will not occur;
- that the partner will always understand;
- that one is finally and permanently desirable;
- that one's own desire will remain stable.

This does not make attachment work naive. It simply locates a limit to what security can accomplish.

---

## 9.4 Differentiation of self and Lacanian separation

Bowen's differentiation of self is the capacity to maintain a coherent position while remaining emotionally connected under pressure.

Lacanian separation is not emotional independence. It concerns the subject's encounter with lack in the Other and the question of what the subject is for the Other's desire.

Useful contrast:

| Bowen | Lacan |
|---|---|
| Differentiation from fusion | Separation from alienating capture in the Other's demand |
| Self-defined principles | Desire not reducible to ego identity |
| Regulation of anxiety in emotional system | Confrontation with lack and object *a* |
| Multigenerational emotional process | Signifier, fantasy, symbolic lineage, desire of the Other |
| Goal can be increased maturity/differentiation | No ideal of a fully autonomous, integrated ego |

The two can illuminate different dimensions of the same event.

---

## 9.5 Object relations “object” and object *a*

This distinction is essential.

In object relations theory, “object” usually refers to a person or internalized representation of a person in relation to the self.

Lacan's object *a* is not the partner, not a memory representation, and not an internal person-object. It is the object-cause of desire, a remainder produced through the subject's entry into the symbolic order and separation.

In couple life, a partner can be made to incarnate object *a* for the subject, but the partner is never reducible to it.

This helps explain why a person can be desired for something they do not knowingly possess and why desire can fade when the partner is treated as fully known or possessed.

---

## 9.6 Projective identification and fantasy

Projective identification describes a dynamic in which disowned aspects of self are located in another, who may then be induced to experience or enact them.

Lacanian fantasy is commonly represented as $ ◊ a: the barred subject in relation to object *a*. Fantasy organizes the subject's position in relation to desire and the Other.

These are not equivalent.

A productive comparison asks:

- Object-relational reading: What affect or self-state is being disowned and placed in the partner?
- Lacanian reading: What scene or position is repeatedly staged so that the partner comes to occupy a particular object-place for the subject?

---

## 9.7 Communication and speech

Many couple therapies aim to improve communication. This can include clearer requests, reflective listening, validation, problem-solving, and reduced criticism.

Lacan's emphasis on speech goes further. Speech is not merely information transfer. The subject says more and less than intended. Slips, repetitions, ambiguities, metaphors, omissions, contradictions, and choices of signifier matter.

A couple can therefore become technically better communicators without touching the unconscious logic of their conflict.

Conversely, psychoanalytic attention to signifiers is not an excuse for poor communication. A couple may benefit greatly from learning not to shout, interrupt, threaten, mind-read, or make contemptuous global accusations.

The useful stance is additive rather than competitive.

---

## 9.8 Shared meaning and the Symbolic

Gottman's “shared meaning” concerns the couple's rituals, values, roles, goals, and sense of a life together.

The Lacanian Symbolic is much broader and more structural. It is not the couple's jointly authored culture. It is the order of language and signifying relations into which subjects are born and through which kinship, law, identity, and desire are mediated.

Shared meaning can therefore be viewed as a local symbolic construction, but it should not be identified with the Symbolic order itself.

---

## 9.9 Negative cycle and repetition

Attachment and behavioral models map recurrent sequences. Lacanian analysis adds the dimension of repetition beyond conscious intention.

A repeated cycle may persist because it:

- secures a familiar subjective position;
- produces certainty about the Other;
- protects against a more disturbing uncertainty;
- sustains a fantasy;
- permits a specific form of jouissance;
- stages an old signifying question in a new relationship.

This does not mean that every repeated argument contains hidden pleasure in an ordinary sense. Jouissance names a form of satisfaction that can coexist with suffering.

---

## 9.10 Acceptance and castration

Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy's emphasis on accepting enduring differences can be productively compared with Lacanian castration.

The overlap is limited but useful. Both can undermine the fantasy that enough effort will produce total complementarity.

The difference is that IBCT's acceptance is a clinical strategy for reducing coercive struggles around differences. Lacanian castration concerns the structural limit imposed by signification and the impossibility of being or possessing the object that would complete the Other.

In a couple context, the Lacanian question is:

> What would I have to give up if I stopped demanding that my partner become the person who would finally make the relationship complete?

---

## 9.11 Anxiety

Mainstream models often define anxiety as physiological and cognitive arousal associated with threat. Lacan's Seminar X famously treats anxiety as not without an object and connects it to the object *a*, the desire of the Other, and disturbances in the coordinates of lack.

The two levels can be used together.

Clinical level:

- identify triggers;
- regulate severe arousal;
- test catastrophic expectations;
- reduce avoidance;
- distinguish genuine danger from imagined threat.

Lacanian level:

- ask what object-position the subject fears occupying for the Other;
- examine what happens when the usual lack or distance disappears;
- ask what the Other wants from the subject;
- identify where desire becomes too proximate.

---

## 9.12 Love, desire, and jouissance

The handbook generally treats good couple functioning as involving some combination of attachment, affection, trust, friendship, intimacy, sexual satisfaction, acceptance, and mutual development.

Lacan allows these to come apart.

One can:

- love without desiring;
- desire without loving;
- enjoy in ways that damage love;
- demand love while avoiding desire;
- seek sexual satisfaction without wanting intimacy;
- idealize the partner while becoming erotically indifferent;
- desire precisely what threatens relational stability.

This differentiation is one of the strongest contributions a Lacanian layer can make to mainstream couple formulations.

# 10. Where the handbook and Lacanian thought converge

The convergence is often at the level of clinical observation rather than theory.

## 10.1 The partner is never perceived neutrally

CBCT speaks of appraisals and schemas. Object relations speaks of representations and projections. Narrative therapy speaks of dominant stories. Lacan speaks of the signifier, imaginary identification, and fantasy.

All reject the naive belief that a partner is simply “seen as they really are.”

## 10.2 Repetition matters

Nearly every model treats repetitive interaction as central. The disagreement concerns what produces repetition.

## 10.3 The dyad is not self-sufficient

Bowen adds triangles and generations. Structural therapy adds subsystems and external boundaries. Narrative therapy adds discourse and power. Intercultural therapy adds cultural worlds. Lacan adds the Other, language, law, and the impossibility of pure two-person immediacy.

## 10.4 Difference cannot simply be abolished

IBCT, Gottman, Bowen, intercultural work, and stepfamily work all insist that durable relationships require ways of living with difference.

## 10.5 Meaning is not reducible to behavior

Even behavioral traditions in this edition have moved beyond crude behaviorism. Contemporary models increasingly include meaning, emotion, history, context, and identity.

## 10.6 Therapist neutrality is complicated

Across models, the therapist is vulnerable to alliance imbalance, enactment, triangulation, countertransference, or imposition of values. Lacan's critique of the analyst as ego ideal or master has a clear comparative relevance here.

---

# 11. Where the handbook and Lacanian thought diverge

## 11.1 Normative models of relational health

Most handbook models ultimately aim at improved satisfaction, security, collaboration, acceptance, communication, intimacy, or functioning. Lacanian analysis is more reluctant to define an ideal relationship form in advance.

## 11.2 The ego

Several therapies aim at stronger self-regulation, coherent identity, secure self-esteem, or mature differentiation. Lacan treats ego coherence with suspicion because the ego is fundamentally imaginary and founded in misrecognition.

## 11.3 Developmental repair

Attachment and object-relations models often conceptualize adult intimacy partly as an opportunity to repair developmental deficits. Lacanian analysis does not promise that a partner or analyst can finally supply what development failed to provide.

## 11.4 The status of lack

Many therapies conceptualize lack as a deficit that can sometimes be met more adequately. Lacan distinguishes contingent deprivation from structural lack. Confusing these leads either to therapeutic pessimism or to impossible expectations of repair.

## 11.5 The goal of understanding

Some models assume that better understanding of self and partner improves functioning. Lacan questions whether knowledge automatically changes jouissance. A subject may understand perfectly and continue to repeat.

## 11.6 Sexuality

Mainstream sex therapy tends to work toward function, pleasure, communication, and reduced anxiety. Lacan's theory asks what sexuality reveals about the impossibility of complementarity and the singular organization of jouissance.

# 13. A two-column formulation method: mainstream clinical map plus Lacanian map

For Notebook use, do not force one framework to swallow the other. Build two formulations in parallel.

| Clinical formulation question | Lacanian formulation question |
|---|---|
| What is the presenting complaint? | Which signifiers organize the complaint? |
| What interaction cycle repeats? | What fantasy or subjective position does the repetition sustain? |
| What does each partner want changed? | What demand is addressed to the Other through that requested change? |
| What genuine needs or practical constraints are present? | Where does desire exceed the satisfiable need? |
| What triggers emotional arousal? | What about the Other's desire becomes anxiety-provoking? |
| What meanings or appraisals are attached to events? | Which master signifiers and unconscious associations confer those meanings? |
| What family-of-origin patterns recur? | How are inherited signifiers, identifications, and the desire of parental Others repeated? |
| What is each partner afraid will happen? | What object-position does each fear occupying for the Other? |
| What does each do to protect themselves? | What defense or fantasy structure is preserved by that action? |
| What third parties stabilize or destabilize the dyad? | What symbolic or fantasmatic function does the third serve? |
| What is the status of sexuality? | How are love, desire, and jouissance articulated or disjoined? |
| What is realistically changeable? | What may need to be assumed as a structural limit rather than eliminated? |
| What outcome does the couple want? | Whose ideal is that outcome, and what desire is served by it? |

This parallel method reduces two opposite errors:

1. psychologizing everything into techniques and skills;
2. Lacanianizing everything into abstract theory while missing concrete relational facts.

# 14. Structured self-analysis protocol derived from the handbook and cross-read through Lacan

This is a reflective framework, not a diagnostic instrument and not a substitute for therapy. It is best used on one recurring interaction at a time.

## Step 1. Describe the event without interpretation

Record:

- what happened;
- what each person said;
- what each person did;
- what happened immediately before;
- what happened immediately after.

Avoid “because,” “always,” “never,” diagnostic labels, or claims about motives.

## Step 2. Identify the interaction loop

Write the cycle in arrows.

Example:

> I ask for reassurance → partner gives a short answer → I interpret shortness as withdrawal → I ask more intensely → partner feels pressured → partner withdraws further → I experience confirmation of abandonment.

Then reverse the perspective:

> Partner perceives repeated questioning → experiences criticism/control → answers briefly → I intensify → partner feels even more controlled → withdraws → sees confirmation that engagement is dangerous.

## Step 3. Separate primary difference from secondary distress

Ask:

- What is the underlying difference?
- Which part is genuinely incompatible or difficult?
- Which part is produced by how we respond to the difference?

Examples of primary difference:

- different preferred frequency of sex;
- different appetite for social contact;
- different spending preferences;
- different need for solitude;
- different attitudes to family involvement;
- different tolerance for uncertainty.

Examples of secondary distress:

- contempt;
- surveillance;
- threats;
- withdrawal;
- repeated accusation;
- catastrophizing;
- retaliatory withholding.

## Step 4. Identify the appraisal

Complete:

> When my partner does X, I take it to mean Y.

Then ask:

- What evidence supports Y?
- What evidence complicates Y?
- What other meanings are possible?
- Why is Y the meaning that has the strongest emotional charge for me?

The final question moves from cognitive work toward psychoanalytic work.

## Step 5. Identify the demand

Complete:

> I want my partner to ______.

Then:

> If they did that, it would prove ______.

Then:

> What would still remain uncertain even if they did it?

This is often where a practical request can be distinguished from an impossible demand for guarantee.

## Step 6. Identify the feared meaning

Complete:

> If my partner does not give me what I ask, I fear it means ______ about me, them, or the relationship.

Common themes include:

- I am not desirable;
- I am not important;
- I am controlled;
- I will be abandoned;
- I am weak;
- I am failing;
- I am replaceable;
- I am trapped;
- I cannot trust my own judgment;
- I will disappear inside the relationship.

Do not assume these are “the truth.” Treat them as signifiers organizing the scene.

## Step 7. Locate the fantasy position

Ask:

- Who am I in this recurring scene?
- Who must the partner be for the scene to work?
- What do I repeatedly prove about myself through the conflict?
- What do I repeatedly prove about the Other?
- What uncertainty would open if the scene stopped repeating?

This is more useful than merely asking, “Where does this come from?”

## Step 8. Look for the third

List all possible thirds:

- child;
- parent;
- ex-partner;
- colleague;
- affair partner;
- friend;
- therapist;
- work;
- alcohol;
- phone;
- pornography;
- money;
- illness;
- ideology;
- family tradition.

Then ask what function the third serves.

## Step 9. Distinguish love, desire, attachment, and jouissance

For the same partner, answer separately:

- What do I love?
- What do I desire?
- What gives me security?
- What irritates me but also draws me back into the same scene?
- What sexual or relational enjoyment do I find difficult to admit?

Do not assume the answers align.

## Step 10. Identify the attempted solution that reproduces the problem

Complete:

> When I feel X, I repeatedly do Y to solve it. The predictable effect is Z.

Ask whether doing less of Y, delaying Y, or doing something structurally different changes the cycle.

## Step 11. Identify what is genuinely negotiable

Separate the issue into:

- behavior that can reasonably change;
- boundaries that must be respected;
- preferences that can be negotiated;
- differences that may have to be accepted;
- conditions that make the relationship unsafe;
- fantasies of total solution that no partner can deliver.

## Step 12. Review after the next recurrence

Do not judge the exercise by whether conflict disappears. Ask:

- Did I recognize the sequence earlier?
- Did I act differently at any point?
- Did my partner respond differently?
- Did the usual meaning feel less certain?
- Was there a moment of choice where previously there was only automatic repetition?

That is often more informative than asking whether the conversation was “successful.”

Write hypotheses rather than verdicts.

## M. What would count as movement?

Include several levels:

- reduced danger or escalation;
- earlier recognition of the cycle;
- less certainty about hostile interpretations;
- greater tolerance of difference;

- clearer choice about staying or separating;

- ability to live with an unresolved point without compulsively reopening it.

# 18. A compact glossary of handbook concepts useful for the Lacanian project

## Acceptance
Reduction of coercive attempts to eliminate enduring difference. Most developed in Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy. Do not equate with resignation or Lacanian castration.

## Attachment injury
An event in which a partner is experienced as unavailable, unresponsive, or betraying at a moment of high attachment need. Relevant especially to Emotionally Focused Couple Therapy and affair work.

## Bid for connection
Gottman term for an overt or subtle attempt to obtain attention, interest, affection, humor, engagement, or emotional contact.

## Circular causality
Systemic principle that A affects B while B simultaneously affects A. Contrasts with simple one-directional causation.

## Countertransference
In the object-relations chapter, the therapist's affective response can be treated as information about relational dynamics and projective processes. Compare carefully with Lacanian transference theory.

## DEEP formulation
Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy formulation organized around Differences, Emotional sensitivities, External stressors, and Patterns of interaction.

## Demand-withdraw
Cycle in which one partner pressures for engagement or change and the other retreats, avoids, or shuts down. Appears across multiple models.

## Differentiation of self
Bowen term for maintaining a self-defined position while staying emotionally connected under relational pressure.

## Emotional flooding
High physiological arousal that impairs productive engagement, emphasized strongly in Gottman work.

## Emotional triangle
Bowen's smallest stable relational unit. A third is recruited when a dyad cannot contain anxiety.

## Empathic joining
Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy intervention that helps partners contact the vulnerable experience underneath polarized conflict.

## Enactment
Structural therapy technique and organizing attitude in which partners interact directly in the session rather than merely report interaction.

## Externalization
Narrative practice of treating the problem as distinct from the person's identity.

## Four Horsemen
Gottman shorthand for criticism, defensiveness, contempt, and stonewalling.

## Functional analysis
Assessment of what behavior does in context, what precedes it, what follows it, and what maintains it. Central to Functional Analytic Couple Therapy and behavioral approaches.

## Gridlock
Gottman term for a perpetual problem that has become entrenched, polarized, and symbolically loaded rather than held in ongoing dialogue.

## Guided discovery
Cognitive-behavioral method of creating experiences that allow clients to revise an interpretation without direct argument.

## Holding environment
Psychodynamic/object-relations concept for a sufficiently safe relational context in which difficult affect and ambivalence can be contained and explored.

## Interpersonal trauma
Framework used in the affair chapter to describe the destabilizing effects of betrayal when fundamental assumptions about partner and relationship collapse.

## Love map
Gottman term for knowledge of the partner's current inner world, concerns, preferences, relationships, stresses, and aspirations.

## Meta-emotion
Beliefs and feelings about having and expressing emotions. Mismatches can become a source of couple conflict.

## Minority stress
Stress generated by stigma, discrimination, rejection, concealment pressures, and structurally unequal social conditions.

## Mutual trap
Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy description of the state in which each partner's attempts to solve the conflict intensify the other's reaction, leaving both feeling stuck.

## Negative cycle
Emotionally Focused Couple Therapy term for a self-reinforcing interaction organized by attachment insecurity and reactive emotion.

## Perpetual problem
Gottman term for an enduring area of difference not expected to be permanently solved.

## Primary distress
Cognitive-Behavioral Couple Therapy term for distress arising from genuine differences or unmet needs.

## Projective identification
Object-relations mechanism in which aspects of self are attributed to and interpersonally induced in another person. Not the same as Lacanian fantasy.

## Secondary distress
Distress produced by maladaptive ways of responding to primary differences, such as attack, withdrawal, coercion, or distortion.

## Sentiment override
Gottman concept describing how the overall emotional climate of the relationship colors interpretation of specific acts.

## Softening
Emotionally Focused Couple Therapy change event in which a partner shifts from defensive or attacking expression toward vulnerable attachment communication that evokes a new response.

## Sound Relationship House
Gottman model integrating friendship, bids, positive perspective, conflict management, dreams, shared meaning, trust, and commitment.

## Splitting
Object-relations defense maintaining polarized good/bad representations. Useful descriptively but theoretically distinct from Lacanian division of the subject.

## Stepfamily architecture
Papernow's concept that stepfamilies begin with structurally unequal insider-outsider positions, prior parent-child bonds, losses, loyalties, and ex-partners already in the system.

## Unified detachment
Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy strategy in which partners step back and examine their conflict pattern jointly rather than remain fused with accusatory positions.

# 19. Interpretive cautions for self-analysis

## 19.1 Do not diagnose the partner from a handbook

Terms such as borderline personality disorder, depression, posttraumatic stress disorder, addiction, attachment style, narcissism, and trauma have clinical meanings. They should not become weapons in couple conflict.

Replace:

> “You are avoidantly attached.”

with:

> “When conflict becomes intense, I notice you tend to withdraw, and I experience that as abandonment.”

The second is more usable clinically and analytically.

## 19.2 Do not turn every practical conflict into an unconscious drama

Some problems are practical:

- unequal workload;
- incompatible schedules;
- financial stress;
- sleep deprivation;
- illness;
- untreated substance misuse;
- lack of childcare;
- different sexual frequency preferences.

Interpretation may matter, but practical arrangements may matter first.

## 19.3 Do not use Lacan to invalidate ordinary needs

The fact that demand cannot finally saturate desire does not mean partners should stop asking for reliability, affection, fidelity, privacy, consideration, or practical support.

## 19.4 Do not use systems language to dissolve responsibility

Circularity does not mean symmetry of responsibility. If one partner is violent, coercive, deceitful, or chronically intoxicated, saying “we co-create the system” can become ethically misleading.

## 19.5 Do not assume relationship preservation is always the goal

Several chapters make clear that successful treatment can include a thoughtful decision to separate.

## 19.6 Do not confuse insight with change

Understanding the cycle is valuable. It does not automatically interrupt it. Repeated behavioral practice, affect regulation, boundary setting, or different speech acts may still be necessary.

## 19.7 Do not make the therapist the final guarantor

A therapist can help formulate, observe, interpret, and intervene. The therapist cannot guarantee that the couple should stay together, that a partner truly loves, or that future betrayal is impossible.

# 22. Final synthesis

The *Clinical Handbook of Couple Therapy* is unusually valuable for a Lacanian couple-research corpus because it makes visible what a Lacanian framework can otherwise leave underdeveloped: the range of rival causal models, the empirical description of interaction, the importance of structured assessment, practical techniques, outcome evidence, and the situations in which safety or comorbidity changes the clinical task.

Its deepest contribution to this project is not any single therapy model. It is the comparative discipline it imposes.

A recurring couple conflict can simultaneously involve:

- a real practical incompatibility;
- a behavioral reinforcement loop;
- a rigid attribution;
- a vulnerable attachment response;
- a family-of-origin repetition;
- a triangle;
- a cultural signifier;
- a sexual or erotic impasse;
- a fantasy about what the partner must be;
- a demand for recognition;
- a form of jouissance;
- and a structural limit no amount of communication can erase.

The intellectual task is not to pick one language and force every fact into it. It is to decide which level explains what, what each theory leaves out, and where one framework is trying to answer a question posed by another.

For Lacanian self-analysis, the most useful practical synthesis is this:

1. **Describe the interaction before interpreting it.**
2. **Identify danger, coercion, psychiatric, substance, medical, or betrayal factors directly.**
3. **Map the circular sequence by which each partner's protection becomes the other's trigger.**
4. **Separate genuine difference from the secondary suffering generated by attempts to abolish difference.**
5. **Identify the explicit request and then ask what proof is being demanded through it.**
6. **Track the signifiers that repeatedly organize the partner as abandoning, engulfing, rejecting, controlling, inadequate, or impossible.**
7. **Ask what fantasy position each subject repeatedly occupies and what certainty the conflict provides.**
8. **Distinguish love, attachment, desire, sexual satisfaction, and jouissance rather than assuming they coincide.**
9. **Recognize what can change through behavior, communication, regulation, and negotiation.**
10. **Recognize what may have to be assumed as difference, lack, uncertainty, or non-complementarity rather than solved.**

That combination makes the handbook highly suitable as a **clinical comparative layer beneath the Lacanian corpus**. It can keep the Lacanian reading concrete without reducing it to mainstream psychotherapy, while Lacan can expose the points where mainstream models tacitly assume that enough regulation, empathy, communication, or mutual understanding could finally make two subjects complementary.
