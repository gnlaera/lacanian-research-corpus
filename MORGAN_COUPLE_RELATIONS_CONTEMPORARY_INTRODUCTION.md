# 1. Why this book is relevant to a Lacanian research notebook

This book is highly relevant as a **clinical and comparative supplement**, but it must be classified correctly. It should not be used as evidence for what Lacan himself taught. Its value lies elsewhere.

First, it offers a systematic vocabulary for thinking about what happens **between two subjects in a couple**, not merely inside either individual. It covers unconscious partner choice, repetition, projection, shared fantasy, shared belief, couple-level defensive systems, narcissistic fusion, alterity, sex, aggression, love, hate, and the relationship as an entity with dynamics of its own. These are precisely the domains in which a Lacanian reader often wants more concrete clinical descriptions.

Second, Morgan repeatedly returns to a central paradox that is unusually compatible with a Lacanian line of inquiry: intimacy does not abolish otherness. The closer one gets to another person, the more one encounters a remainder that cannot be fully known, assimilated, controlled, or made identical to oneself. Morgan approaches this through object relations and link theory. Lacan reaches related problems through the Imaginary, the symbolic Other, desire, fantasy, jouissance, the barred Other, and the non-existence of a pre-given sexual relation. The theories are not interchangeable, but the clinical problem overlaps.

Third, the book is especially useful for **self-analysis of repetitive couple patterns**. Morgan provides concrete ways to ask: What do I unconsciously expect a partner to be? What part do I assign to the other? What do I refuse to know about myself? What shared story do we keep making true? What happens when my partner does not fit the role I need them to occupy? What happens to curiosity when certainty takes over?

Fourth, it is useful for couple therapy because it shifts the unit of attention from two isolated individuals to **the relation they co-create**. This is clinically productive even where a Lacanian analyst would formulate the structure differently.

Finally, the book is useful as a **theoretical contrast**. Lacan's _Seminar IV: The Object Relation_ explicitly challenges the tendency to make a dual subject-object relation the central explanatory model of psychoanalysis. Morgan, by contrast, is located inside traditions that developed precisely from object-relations and link approaches. Reading them together prevents accidental conflation and makes the differences in metapsychology visible.

### Bottom-line classification

- **High value:** couple dynamics, clinical observation, self-reflection, pattern recognition, curiosity about the partner, projection, repetition, fusion, difference, shared couple myths, sexual and emotional impasses.
- **Medium-high value:** comparative psychoanalysis and translation between Lacanian and non-Lacanian clinical languages.
- **Low value as authority:** direct claims about Lacan, Lacanian metapsychology, object _a_, sexuation, the Real, or the analytic act. Morgan is not writing those theories.
- **Main risk:** importing Kleinian/object-relations concepts into Lacan as though they were equivalent concepts. They are not.

# 2. The book's overall model

Morgan organises couple life around three interlocking domains.

## 2.1 The intrapsychic domain: what each partner brings

Each person enters a relationship with an internal world formed by early attachment, fantasy, identification, conflict, sexuality, family narratives, transgenerational material, culture, and prior relationships. The partner is therefore never encountered on a blank slate. One perceives the actual person through expectations, fears, wishes, and remembered or internalised relationships.

The couple can become a place where unresolved material is repeated defensively, but it can also become a setting in which previously split-off or unmanageable parts of the self are gradually encountered and transformed.

## 2.2 The interpersonal or interpsychic domain: what happens between them

The couple generates recurrent patterns that neither partner can adequately explain by looking only at their individual psychology. Partners project into each other, elicit states from each other, occupy complementary roles, construct shared fantasies and beliefs, and create cycles of conflict and reassurance. The relationship can therefore function like a system.

Morgan also stresses that the partner is not merely a screen for projection. The other is a real subject whose difference can resist the role one assigns them. The partner's presence introduces novelty, uncertainty, and what link theorists call interference.

## 2.3 The relationship as a third: what the couple creates that then affects them

Over time, a couple creates patterns, values, myths, prohibitions, ideals, routines, and ways of containing or failing to contain anxiety. These become features of the relationship itself. Morgan treats the couple not merely as A plus B but as A, B, and an emergent relational third.

A healthy development is the capacity to take a reflective position toward this third, to ask not only "what are you doing to me?" but "what are we creating between us?" Morgan calls this a **couple state of mind**. A further development is the **creative couple**, in which difference can generate something new instead of being eliminated.

## 2.4 The central dynamic running through the book

Across all three domains, the recurring polarity is:

**certainty / fusion / sameness / control**  
versus  
**curiosity / separateness / otherness / uncertainty / creation**.

The book's most useful practical proposition is that couple difficulty often intensifies when anxiety converts the unknown other into a known object. The partner is then treated less as a subject to be encountered and more as someone whose motives, feelings, and role are already presumed to be known.

# 3. Introduction: the unconscious between two people

Morgan defines a psychoanalytic approach to couple relations by its focus on unconscious processes operating not only in each individual but **between** them. She uses the term _interpsychic_ for the unconscious field that emerges through interaction.

Her opening paradox is fundamental: greater intimacy creates greater exposure to the partner's otherness. Genuine intimacy therefore cannot mean perfect access to the other's interior. It requires tolerating the fact that the other is only partially knowable and maintaining curiosity about them.

Morgan draws on Bion's L, H, and K, meaning love, hate, and knowing. Love and hate are both inherent in intimate relationships. Development depends less on eliminating hate than on the capacity of a predominantly loving bond to contain aggressive and hateful states without destroying the relation.

The introduction also establishes several developmental ideas:

- Human beings begin life in relationships and develop an internal preconception of linking.
- The first caregiver relationship provides an early model of coupling.
- Recognition of a parental or symbolic couple creates a triangular perspective.
- Adult coupling requires the capacity to be both a participant in the couple and an observer of the couple.
- Morgan calls this reflective capacity a **couple state of mind**.

The book compares two major traditions:

1. **The Tavistock model**, developing object-relations theory for couple work.
2. **The link perspective**, developed in Latin America and France, emphasizing the link as a new psychic reality generated between subjects.

Morgan does not try to collapse these schools into one theory. Her position is that they can illuminate complementary dimensions of couple life.

# 4. Chapter 1: An overview of the field

## 4.1 Historical origins

Morgan traces the psychoanalytic study of couples to parallel developments from the 1940s onward.

In Britain, the Family Discussion Bureau, later Tavistock Relationships, and the Tavistock Clinic developed a psychoanalytic couple approach from object-relations theory. Henry Dicks, Lily Pincus, Kathleen Bannister, and later Tavistock clinicians progressively reformulated individual analytic concepts for the couple as a unit.

In Argentina and the Río de la Plata region, Enrique Pichon-Rivière developed a theory of links later elaborated by Isidoro Berenstein and Janine Puget. In France, René Käes developed a related metapsychology of links and groups.

## 4.2 The Tavistock object-relations orientation

The Tavistock tradition assumes an internal world of relationships that shapes perception of the external world. The inner world is not closed. New external experiences can modify it.

Individual concepts were transformed into couple concepts:

- unconscious fantasy became **shared unconscious fantasy**;
- projective identification became a **couple projective system**;
- internal conflict could become a complementary division of psychological labour between partners;
- the couple itself became the relevant clinical object.

The model is therefore not simply "two individual analyses in the same room". The relationship is treated as a psychological system.

Morgan identifies a cluster of Tavistock concepts that recur throughout the book:

- unconscious choice of partner and couple fit;
- couple projective system;
- joint marital personality and unconscious complementarity;
- narcissistic relating versus separateness and otherness;
- shared unconscious fantasy;
- unconscious beliefs about being a couple;
- couple transference;
- the relationship as psychological container;
- marital triangle;
- couple state of mind;
- creative couple.

## 4.3 The link perspective

The link perspective goes further in refusing to reduce the other to an object represented inside the subject. When two subjects encounter one another, their relation produces something new that did not pre-exist in either person.

Key ideas include:

- the **link** as a psychic reality in its own right;
- the partner as another subject, not only an object of fantasy;
- **presence**, meaning the impact of the real existence of the other on the self;
- **interference**, meaning the disturbance introduced by an other who does not fit one's anticipations;
- **uncertainty**, because the result of two subjectivities meeting is not fully predictable;
- unconscious alliances that stabilize the link and sometimes defend it against threatening material.

## 4.4 Similarities and differences between the schools

The Tavistock perspective often begins from internal objects, projection, repetition, and developmental working-through. The link tradition emphasizes the new event created between subjects and the irreducible novelty of the other.

Morgan's synthesis is pragmatic: both processes can coexist. We seek familiarity, identify with a partner, and repeat earlier patterns, but the partner also resists being fully absorbed into those repetitions.

## 4.5 Three domains

Morgan summarises couple psychoanalysis as the study of:

1. the individual/intrapsychic;
2. the interpersonal/interpsychic;
3. the relationship or link as a third.

She also describes these as past, present, and future:

- **past:** what each person brings;
- **present:** the dynamics of difference and interaction;
- **future:** what the couple can newly create.

### Practical value for self-analysis

When a conflict repeats, do not ask only "who is right?" Ask three separate questions:

- What old expectation is each of us bringing?
- What pattern are we producing right now?
- What has our relationship itself come to demand, prohibit, or expect from us?

# 5. Chapter 2: The intrapsychic and the individual within a relationship

## 5.1 Couple formation as continuing psychic development

Morgan rejects the idea that psychological development is complete before the adult relationship begins. Entering a couple can reactivate primitive anxieties around dependence, abandonment, merger, separateness, inclusion, exclusion, jealousy, and sexuality.

The relationship can function in two opposite ways:

- as a developmental setting in which difficult parts of the self can be encountered and reintegrated;
- as a defensive system that keeps disowned parts located in the partner.

The other person's difference can also alter the self in genuinely new ways. The partner is therefore not merely a repetition of the past.

## 5.2 The first relationship: holding and containment

Morgan uses Winnicott and Bion to describe early relational development.

From Winnicott she takes the importance of a sufficiently reliable holding environment that permits continuity of being.

From Bion she takes the model of containment. Primitive, unprocessed emotional states are received and metabolised by the caregiver, then returned in a more thinkable form. Over time the infant internalises a capacity to process emotion.

For adult couple life, the implication is that partners differ in how well they can:

- experience intense affect without evacuating it;
- receive another's distress without being overwhelmed;
- distinguish their own feelings from the other's;
- return emotional material in a more thinkable form.

## 5.3 Clinical example: Lucy and Joe

Lucy and Joe struggle to speak without feeling psychologically endangered. Even setting up a conversation becomes highly controlled. Lucy experiences Joe's divided attention as erasure of her existence. Both appear desperate to be heard but have little confidence that emotional material can be safely received, processed, and returned.

Morgan interprets their interaction as reflecting failures of early containment. Conversation becomes less an exchange of minds and more a battle over who must hold whose unprocessed experience. The couple also reproduce this dynamic with the therapist, wanting the therapist to take in their material but distrusting what may come back.

**Usefulness:** this case is an excellent model for distinguishing "communication problems" from a more fundamental problem of emotional digestion. Sometimes the issue is not missing communication technique but the fact that receiving the other's mind feels dangerous.

## 5.4 The Oedipal situation and becoming three

Morgan follows post-Kleinian thinking in emphasizing a developmental shift from being inside a dyad to recognizing a couple from outside. The child discovers that the primary caregiver has relationships, interests, and attachments that do not include the child.

This creates a **third position**. The subject can be inside a relationship and also imagine it from outside. In adult couple life this becomes the capacity to observe the couple one is participating in.

This developmental achievement also brings painful themes that reappear throughout life:

- exclusion;
- jealousy;
- loss of exclusive possession;
- the presence of a third;
- rivalry;
- the fact that the loved other has desires outside the self.

The birth of a child, affairs, open relationships, polyamory, and grandparenthood can all reactivate such dynamics in different forms.

## 5.5 Adolescence

Adolescence intensifies bodily sexuality, separation from parents, dependency conflicts, experimentation, and uncertainty about sexual identity and orientation. Morgan treats the capacity to enter an adult couple as requiring some ability to possess one's own mind and body while tolerating an intimate bond with another.

## 5.6 The creative couple

Morgan's positive developmental model is not perfect harmony. It is the capacity to allow two different minds to meet without knowing in advance what the result will be.

The creative couple can:

- tolerate difference long enough for thought to occur;
- remain connected without forcing sameness;
- bear uncertainty;
- allow prior assumptions to break down;
- generate an outcome neither partner could have produced alone.

The relationship then becomes a psychic resource rather than merely a source of reassurance or conflict.

### Self-reflection questions from Chapter 2

- Which emotional states do I find almost impossible to hold without making my partner responsible for them?
- When distressed, do I want my partner to understand me, agree with me, rescue me, regulate me, or simply remain present?
- What does my partner's independent life stir in me: curiosity, relief, envy, abandonment, humiliation, jealousy, fear?
- Can I observe our pattern while still feeling my own position, or do I become entirely absorbed in the immediate dyad?
- What becomes possible in this relationship that was not possible for me alone?

# 6. Chapter 3: The interpersonal, Part 1: unconscious partner choice, projective systems, transference, interference and uncertainty

## 6.1 "The patient is the couple"

Morgan's starting point is that couple psychoanalysis investigates the relation jointly produced by both partners. The clinical object is not reducible to either member separately.

## 6.2 Unconscious choice of partner

Conscious attraction coexists with unconscious selection. A partner may be compelling because something in them feels familiar, completes a psychological configuration, carries a repudiated part of the self, or provides a setting in which an unresolved relationship can be repeated.

The language of finding one's "other half" can therefore express a real psychological process: the other seems to contain something experienced as missing from the self.

## 6.3 Projective identification: several distinct processes

Morgan is careful to distinguish different uses of projective identification.

In its more defensive form, an intolerable aspect of self is split off and attributed to the other. The relationship then helps maintain the split.

In Bion-influenced formulations, projection can also be a primitive communication. One partner unconsciously induces or evokes a state in the other because it cannot yet be processed internally. If the recipient can bear and transform the state, the original partner may later be able to reclaim it in a more manageable form.

This distinction is crucial in couple work. The same surface pattern, such as one partner becoming angry and the other calm, can have very different functions.

## 6.4 Couple projective system and developmental complementarity

A couple can form a stable division of psychic functions. One carries vulnerability, another competence; one anger, another depression; one spontaneity, another control. Such complementarity is not necessarily pathological.

It becomes developmental when each partner can gradually re-own capacities that were temporarily carried by the other. It becomes defensive when the relationship is organised to prevent either person from changing roles.

## 6.5 Defensive couple systems

Morgan describes several failure modes:

- one partner projects a feeling and then stimulates it in the other so it remains located there;
- both project the same intolerable affect into each other, creating escalation;
- partners polarise around a shared conflict, each carrying one side;
- one partner takes over capacities projected by the other and resists giving them back;
- the couple repeatedly recreates an early painful relation while believing they are trying to avoid it.

## 6.6 Clinical example: Matt and Abe

Matt and Abe both struggle with anxiety. Each experiences the other as the anxious one and then becomes anxious in response. Their attempts to identify, regulate, or expose the other's anxiety increase the shared level of anxiety.

Morgan uses the case to show how conflict can become a circuit of projection and re-projection. Speech ceases to be primarily communicative and becomes action. Each tries to place intolerable affect in the other.

**Self-analysis clue:** when an argument escalates, ask whether the spoken content is the real issue or whether each person is trying to relocate a feeling.

## 6.7 Couple transference and repetition

Each partner brings transferential expectations formed by early objects and previous relationships. Repetition can be strikingly paradoxical. A person may consciously choose a partner who appears opposite to a feared parent and still end up recreating a psychologically similar structure.

Morgan's point is not that repetition is fate. The problem is repetition without recognition and transformation.

## 6.8 Interference and uncertainty: the other refuses the script

Link theory supplies a counterweight to repetition. The partner is never only the object expected by the self. Their separate subjectivity interferes with the script.

**Interference** names what happens when the presence of the other produces something not assimilable to prior expectations. The couple must then do something with the new situation.

**Uncertainty** follows from this. No relationship can be completely known in advance because the link is produced through encounter.

This is one of the most useful concepts in the whole book for a Lacanian reader. It prevents a reductive interpretation in which every new event is treated as merely a disguised repetition of childhood.

### Self-reflection questions from Chapter 3

- Which role does my partner repeatedly end up occupying in my story?
- What feeling do I most insist belongs to them rather than me?
- Which capacity do I admire or resent in them because I have difficulty owning it myself?
- What do I do when they refuse the role I implicitly assign them?
- When I say "you always...", am I describing the present, a repetition, or a script that I help produce?
- What in this relationship remains genuinely new and not reducible to my past?

# 7. Chapter 4: The interpersonal, Part 2: shared unconscious fantasy, beliefs, alliances, and curiosity

## 7.1 Unconscious fantasy and reality testing

Morgan uses the Kleinian notion that unconscious fantasy accompanies conscious life and influences how reality is interpreted. The same event can therefore be lived in radically different ways by two partners.

Healthy development does not mean the disappearance of fantasy. It means fantasy remains modifiable by experience. Reality can surprise the subject.

A problem arises when emotional conviction becomes so strong that external evidence cannot modify the internal story.

## 7.2 Shared unconscious fantasy

A couple can build a jointly sustained fantasy. The partners' anxieties and expectations fit together and produce a shared defensive organisation.

Examples of the underlying logic include:

- dependence means abandonment;
- separateness means rejection;
- disagreement means loss of love;
- sex means intrusion or engulfment;
- vulnerability means weakness;
- conflict means the relationship is failing.

The couple may then create routines that protect them from the feared outcome while simultaneously preventing corrective experience.

## 7.3 Shared defence

A shared defence is the relational machinery built to keep a shared anxiety from being encountered. It may provide stability but at the price of constriction.

For example, a couple afraid of abandonment may organise constant proximity. The arrangement reduces anxiety but may also prevent independent development, erotic distance, and curiosity.

## 7.4 Unconscious beliefs

Morgan follows Ronald Britton in distinguishing flexible fantasy from **unconscious belief**. An unconscious belief is felt less like a hypothesis and more like a fact about reality or about what relationships inherently are.

Typical couple beliefs include:

- partners should tell each other everything;
- true love should eliminate serious conflict;
- a loving partner should automatically know what the other needs;
- if one must ask, the response is no longer meaningful;
- misunderstanding implies bad intention;
- a good couple must be emotionally attuned most of the time;
- ambivalence toward a partner or child means something is wrong.

The important feature is not the proposition itself but the **certainty** attached to it. The belief is defended as though questioning it threatens identity or the relationship.

## 7.5 Clinical illustration: support as an unquestionable entitlement

Morgan describes a husband who assumes that in a proper couple his wife must provide a particular form of emotional support. The analyst experiences pressure to validate this as self-evident. The clinical interest lies in the certainty and the lack of space for inquiry.

The point is not that partners should not support one another. The point is that a value can become an unconscious law that cannot be examined.

## 7.6 Proleptic imagination

The **proleptic imagination** describes a mode in which an anticipated story is experienced as already true. The imagined event and reality collapse into one another.

Morgan's theatre example is highly useful: a partner is late; anxiety becomes certainty of abandonment or betrayal; the actual explanation arrives but cannot dislodge the emotional fact already formed. The evening is then lived as though the abandonment occurred.

This helps explain why some arguments feel scripted. The couple already knows the ending and unconsciously moves toward it.

## 7.7 Unconscious alliances

René Käes' notion of unconscious alliance describes relational agreements that allow desires to be realised and threatening material to remain repressed, denied, or disavowed. The alliance helps preserve the bond but may constrain the subjects within it.

The concept overlaps with shared defence but is broader. It treats the link itself as a structure that helps determine what can and cannot be known.

## 7.8 Curiosity as an antidote to certainty

For Morgan and Philip Stokoe, curiosity is central. Anxiety favours certainty because certainty temporarily stabilises the self. Curiosity re-opens the possibility that one's belief is not identical with reality.

In practical terms, curiosity means being able to ask:

- What else might this mean?
- What is the other actually saying rather than what I expect them to say?
- What am I frightened I would discover if my current explanation were wrong?
- Which facts do I ignore because they do not fit our couple story?

### High-value insight for self-analysis

The crucial distinction is not **true belief versus false belief**. It is **belief that can be examined versus certainty that cannot be examined**.

# 8. Chapter 5: The interpersonal, Part 3: narcissism and alterity

## 8.1 Intimacy requires both togetherness and separateness

Morgan treats mature intimacy as a tension rather than a fusion. The couple needs enough togetherness to sustain attachment and enough separateness for each partner to remain a subject with a mind, desire, and perspective of their own.

Difficulties arise at either pole:

- closeness can be sought as merger or feared as engulfment;
- separateness can be experienced as healthy autonomy or as abandonment;
- difference can be welcomed as interesting or attacked as disloyalty;
- agreement can be comforting or become a condition for psychological survival.

## 8.2 Narcissism versus "marriage" as a psychic state

James Fisher uses _marriage_ not merely as a legal institution but as a psychic capacity to tolerate the oscillation between oneness and separation.

In narcissistic relating, the other is wanted primarily as an attuned extension of the self. Their independent existence becomes difficult to bear. What appears to be closeness may therefore conceal intolerance of alterity.

The developmental task is not self-erasure. It is being able to retain one's own experience while also granting reality to the other's different experience.

## 8.3 Relating and non-relating

Warren Colman adds an important corrective: healthy relationships also require periods of non-relating. Solitude, privacy, independent interests, and the ability to be alone in the presence of another are not failures of intimacy.

This is clinically useful because some couples interpret every withdrawal or independent activity as a relational injury. Morgan's framework asks whether the couple can distinguish ordinary psychic space from abandonment or rejection.

## 8.4 Alterity, alienness, and presence

The link perspective introduces one of the strongest ideas in the book: something in the other cannot be converted into the self's terms. Even in a very close relationship, a remainder of the other stays alien.

**Presence** names the impact the other's existence has on me. Belonging to a relationship changes subjectivity. I cannot remain exactly who I was because another subject affects me.

**Interference** names the disruption produced when the other does not fit my prior categories.

**Imposition** is a defensive response to that disruption. Instead of being changed by the encounter, I attempt to force the other back into similarity. I insist on defining them, their motives, or what the relationship means.

This is a very useful way to understand controlling exchanges that are subtler than overt domination. Imposition often appears as certainty about the partner: "I know what you really think", "you don't mean that", "you only say that because...".

## 8.5 Imperfect attunement

Morgan rejects the fantasy that healthy intimacy produces continuous mutual understanding. Misunderstanding is inevitable.

A secure relationship is marked not by perfect attunement but by the capacity to:

- survive misunderstanding;
- tolerate disagreement;
- ask rather than presume;
- repair after misattunement;
- accept the limits of one's access to the other.

Britton's formulation is particularly useful: when a person has little expectation of being understood, the need for agreement can become absolute. Disagreement then feels less like a difference of view and more like psychic annihilation.

## 8.6 Narcissistic pacts

Morgan groups several unconscious couple arrangements under the idea of **narcissistic pacts**. These are relational solutions to the anxiety created by difference.

### Pact 1: oneness

The early state of falling in love often contains merger and idealisation. In most couples this gradually yields to a more reality-based relation in which difference can be tolerated.

In a projective gridlock, however, the couple tries to preserve psychological oneness. Each lives inside the other's mind through projection and identification. Conflict may be reduced temporarily, but so are difference, surprise, and desire. Eventually one partner may experience the relation as claustrophobic. Withdrawal then triggers panic in the other because so much of the self has been psychically lodged in the partner.

### Pact 2: sado-masochistic complementarity

One partner may occupy certainty, authority, or self-definition while the other yields their own mind. The more dependent partner gains security by submitting to the other's certainty. The dominant partner gains protection against separateness by defining the other.

This is not simply a strong personality paired with a weak one. It is a mutually stabilised system in which each position supports the other.

### Clinical example: Mario and Lula

Mario insists that he knows what Lula really wants about a holiday. Lula begins the session with a clear separate preference but progressively loses confidence in her own position. Morgan experiences the interaction as though Mario's mind is entering and replacing Lula's.

The clinical point is the couple's difficulty tolerating two minds. When the therapist introduces a third, separate perspective, Mario becomes angry. Difference itself is felt as a threat.

### Pact 3: one person occupies the psychic space

Drawing on Britton, Morgan describes couples in which one partner cannot find a place outside the other's psychic reality while the other cannot find a place inside it. Another version involves one partner's inner world colonising the relationship while the other's remains secret, undeveloped, or excluded.

## 8.7 Curiosity versus possession

Morgan repeatedly contrasts curiosity with narcissism. Curiosity accepts that the other is not already known. Intrusiveness seeks to abolish the unknown by entering, defining, or possessing the other's mind.

This distinction is especially useful in long relationships. Familiarity can become a defence against discovery. The sentence "I know exactly how you are" may contain knowledge, but it may also function as a prohibition on the partner becoming different.

### Self-reflection questions from Chapter 5

- How much disagreement can I tolerate before I feel the relationship itself is threatened?
- When my partner says something unexpected, do I inquire or explain it away?
- Do I treat privacy as autonomy or secrecy?
- Do I confuse understanding with agreement?
- Where do I presume I know my partner better than they know themselves?
- What kind of difference in them is hardest for me to permit?
- Which version of "us" depends on one of us not changing?

# 9. Chapter 6: The couple relationship as a third

## 9.1 The core proposition

A couple produces something that cannot be reduced to either individual. Different psychoanalytic traditions name this third in different ways, but the shared idea is that once relational patterns are created, they begin to shape the people who created them.

This third can be:

- defensive;
- developmental;
- containing;
- persecutory;
- idealising;
- rigid;
- creative.

## 9.2 The analytic third

Thomas Ogden's analytic third refers to a subjectivity generated between analyst and analysand that is not simply either person's private experience. Morgan extends the relevance of this idea to couples: each person remains separate, but there is also a field generated between them.

## 9.3 Transitional space and creative illusion

Drawing on Winnicott, several writers argue that mature love need not eliminate illusion entirely. A secure relationship can include moments of merger, play, fantasy, and creative unintegration without collapsing into destructive fusion.

This is important because a purely sober model of mature love can become lifeless. The issue is not illusion versus reality in absolute terms. It is whether the couple can move between them without losing reality testing.

## 9.4 The third position

Britton's third position is the capacity to observe a relation from outside while remaining inside it. Morgan makes this foundational to couple development.

A two-person conflict often has the form:

> what are you doing to me, or failing to do for me?

A third-position question becomes:

> what are we doing to each other, and what are we producing between us?

This is not a denial of responsibility. It adds another level of responsibility.

## 9.5 Thirds described in the object-relations and related traditions

Morgan surveys multiple versions of the relational third.

### Couple projective system / joint marital personality

The couple may distribute psychological functions between them. Once stabilised, this division itself becomes part of the relationship's identity.

### Selfdyad

From self psychology, the couple can form a conjoint system in which each supports mirroring, idealising, or twinship functions for the other.

### Collusion

Partners may take opposite sides of a shared conflict. The polarity looks like difference but is actually a coordinated defensive system.

### Joint superego and joint ego ideal

The couple develops shared standards, prohibitions, ideals, and an implicit image of what the relationship should be. These can support commitment and ethical concern or become punitive and restrictive.

### Relational third / malignant third

A relationship may carry unresolved trauma brought by one or both partners. A traumatic pattern can become a third presence that repeatedly organises the couple.

### Shared unconscious fantasy and belief

Repeated shared expectations become background structures through which events are interpreted.

### Marriage or relationship as psychological container

A stable relationship can become a psychic container for difficult states, allowing feelings to be held without immediate action or evacuation.

### Marital triangle

The relationship itself can be represented as a third point in addition to the two partners. This permits consideration of the needs of the self, the partner, and the relationship.

## 9.6 Couple state of mind

Morgan's signature concept is the **couple state of mind**. It is both a stance the therapist brings and a capacity the partners can develop.

It includes the ability to:

- hold both partners in mind simultaneously;
- think about the relationship as a unit;
- move between first-person experience and observation;
- recognise recurrent patterns without reducing either partner to the pattern;
- ask what the couple is creating;
- recover from dyadic blame.

## 9.7 Clinical example: Abby and Elish

Abby and Elish repeatedly argue about parenting. Anxiety about their children is quickly converted into blame: one is too permissive, the other too controlling.

In the important session Morgan describes, the couple begins to recognise that the familiar argument is something they make together under stress. Abby can see that Elish's permissiveness evokes a controlling part of her that she dislikes. The couple starts to speak about the difficulty they share rather than simply identify the defective partner.

This shift marks the emergence of a couple state of mind.

## 9.8 The creative couple

The creative couple goes beyond observation. Once difference can be tolerated, the partners can allow their perspectives to interact without forcing a predetermined synthesis.

The couple's strength then lies not in permanent agreement but in the capacity to generate new forms when old arrangements no longer work.

### A compact diagnostic grid for the relational third

Ask:

1. What is the relationship currently making easy for us?
2. What is it making difficult or unthinkable?
3. What role does each person repeatedly occupy?
4. Which feelings circulate easily, and which must be located in only one partner?
5. Which shared ideals govern us?
6. What happens when one person changes?
7. Can we observe the pattern together?
8. Can the relationship generate something new, or only restore the old equilibrium?

# 10. Chapter 7: Sex and sexuality in couple relations

## 10.1 Morgan's broad frame

Morgan treats sexuality as simultaneously psychological, relational, biological, neurological, cultural, and social. She discusses sexual desire, loss of desire, gender identity, sexual orientation, same-sex couples, monogamy, open relationships, polyamory, social media, and pornography.

This chapter is deliberately eclectic. For a Lacanian notebook it should be used descriptively, not as a substitute for Lacanian theories of desire, drive, jouissance, sexuation, or the sexual non-relation.

## 10.2 Sexual desire and the loss of desire

Morgan surveys several explanations rather than reducing loss of desire to one cause.

### Neurobiological account

Different neural and hormonal systems contribute to lust, romantic attraction, and attachment. Early intense sexual attraction does not necessarily persist unchanged as secure attachment develops.

The practical implication is important: a decline from early intensity is not by itself proof that a relationship is psychologically defective.

### Enigma and mirroring

Following Laplanche-influenced writers, sexuality is treated as containing an enigmatic element that is not fully mirrored or integrated in early development. Adult sexuality may therefore involve an attempt to elaborate something in the self that remains partly foreign.

This account emphasizes why another person's desire can be so important and so destabilizing.

### The core complex

The core complex describes an oscillation between longing for merger and fear of engulfment, followed by withdrawal and fear of abandonment. Morgan uses this to understand sexual and emotional approach-avoidance patterns.

Sex can therefore carry contradictory meanings:

- closeness;
- surrender;
- invasion;
- proof of love;
- autonomy;
- dependence;
- reassurance;
- transgression.

### Sex, aggression, and transgression

Morgan surveys writers who argue that aggression is part of sexual excitement. Penetration, engulfment, pursuit, surrender, and transgression all involve boundary crossing.

The crucial distinction is whether aggression is contained by recognition, concern, and love or whether it becomes domination and objectification.

## 10.3 Desire may diminish when difference is eliminated

The chapter contains an important recurring theme: if the couple's bond becomes organised around complete merger, aggression and difference may be excluded. This can also remove conditions that sustain desire.

Desire therefore cannot simply be equated with attachment or harmony.

## 10.4 Internalised homophobia

Morgan describes how social stigma can be internalised and become self-hatred, shame, or a punitive couple superego in same-sex relationships. The partner may then become the target of feelings originally directed toward the self.

### Clinical example: Jim and Eric

Jim and Eric lose sexual desire after moving in together and choosing monogamy. Their previous open relationship had, at an unconscious level, been associated with validating their gay identity. Monogamy becomes linked to the idea of becoming a "proper couple", which also activates shame about not being a proper couple in heteronormative terms.

Their frequent arguments are understood as a mutual attempt to place the feeling of being wrong or defective into the other. The case shows how a sexual arrangement can carry symbolic meanings far beyond its explicit rules.

## 10.5 Monogamy, open relationships, and polyamory

Morgan does not present one arrangement as inherently psychologically superior. Instead she asks what function a structure serves for a particular couple.

Questions include:

- Is monogamy experienced as commitment, safety, convention, control, or proof of exclusivity?
- Is openness an expression of sexual freedom, a jointly chosen structure, a defence against intimacy, a response to mismatched desire, or something else?
- What counts as betrayal in this particular relationship?
- In open relationships, is emotional attachment outside the primary couple more threatening than sexual contact?
- How is jealousy understood and managed?
- Is one member structurally more privileged than another?

The clinically useful principle is to analyze the couple's unconscious meaning of the arrangement rather than assuming meaning from the arrangement itself.

## 10.6 Social media, pornography, and the development of sexuality

Morgan highlights two concerns.

First, idealised online bodies and identities can intensify certainty about how one should look, desire, perform, or identify, especially during adolescent development.

Second, pornography can bypass the uncertainty of another person's desire. A digital sexual object can be selected, controlled, and replaced. A real partner may not want what one wants, at the same time, in the same way.

The chapter uses mentalization to describe a capacity essential to sexuality: being able to imagine that one's own desire does not determine the other's desire.

### Practical questions about sex in a couple

- What does sex mean to each of us beyond physical pleasure?
- When desire falls, what else has changed in our relation?
- Does closeness increase desire, decrease it, or both?
- Is sex used to repair, reassure, merge, dominate, avoid talking, prove love, or confirm attractiveness?
- What differences in desire are difficult to acknowledge?
- What forms of aggression or transgression enliven desire, and where are the ethical and consent boundaries?
- What does the structure of our relationship symbolize to each of us?
- Can I imagine my partner's desire as genuinely separate from mine?

# 11. Chapter 8: Love, hate, and creativity

## 11.1 Falling in love versus mature love

Morgan distinguishes, without creating an absolute binary, between the initial in-love state and more mature forms of love.

The in-love state often involves:

- idealisation;
- intense sexual desire;
- merger;
- heightened emotional experience;
- selective perception;
- the fantasy of finding the missing or perfect other.

Mature love involves more recognition of the other's separateness, limitations, needs, ambivalence, and unknowability.

The transition is difficult because the loss of the ideal object can reactivate earlier experiences of loss.

## 11.2 Mourning idealisation

A couple must often mourn the relationship they imagined they had or thought they should have. If the idealised form cannot be relinquished, disappointment becomes chronic because the actual partner is continually measured against a fantasy.

Morgan also preserves a place for creative illusion. Mature love does not require eliminating play, merger, fantasy, or moments of idealisation. It requires knowing, at some level, that the illusion is not the whole reality.

## 11.3 Mature love and the depressive position

Morgan's object-relations frame treats mature love as involving capacities associated with the depressive position:

- integration of good and bad aspects of self and other;
- concern;
- gratitude;
- guilt that can lead to reparation rather than collapse;
- tolerance of ambivalence;
- acceptance of separateness;
- ability to forgive and seek forgiveness;
- continuing curiosity.

But Morgan also emphasizes that no mature state is permanently achieved. Relationships repeatedly move into fragmentation, conflict, idealisation, hatred, merger, and repair.

## 11.4 Love can itself feel dangerous

For people with histories of unreliable containment, trauma, or insecure attachment, dependency and vulnerability may be terrifying. Repetitive quarrelling can function as a defence against being loved or needing another.

Hate may also defend against love when love is experienced as exposing the self to loss.

## 11.5 Love, hate, and knowing

Using Bion's L, H, and K, Morgan argues that love and hate need to become thinkable. A couple that insists only on love may become less alive because hatred goes underground and returns as detachment, control, provocation, retaliation, sexual withdrawal, or chronic grievance.

The aim is not to celebrate hostility. It is to keep aggression representable and containable rather than dissociated or enacted.

## 11.6 Violence

Morgan distinguishes ordinary aggression and hatred from violence. She reviews four forms of intimate partner violence described in the literature:

- coercive controlling violence;
- violent resistance;
- situational couple violence;
- separation-instigated violence.

This distinction matters because not all violent dynamics have the same structure or clinical implications.

### Situational escalation

In some couples, arguments escalate through forceful projection. Each loses the ability to contain intense feeling, and action replaces thought.

### Clinical example: the split ownership of anger

Morgan describes a couple in which the wife appears to be the angry partner while the husband appears calm, powerless, and reasonable. Over time it becomes visible that he also carries significant anger but keeps it split off, subtly provoking and amplifying his wife's anger. She becomes the carrier of the couple's aggression.

The example shows why a simple surface description such as "she is the angry one" may miss the couple system.

### Containment, mirroring, mentalization, and attachment

Morgan uses several developmental models to explain why intense affect may become bodily action rather than thought. Where affect has not been reliably contained or mentalized, the person may have difficulty recognizing their own mental state, representing the other's, or maintaining awareness of two minds under stress.

She also reviews research associating some insecure attachment patterns with abusive dynamics.

### Social and cultural context

The chapter does not treat violence as purely intrapsychic. It notes the role of cultural misogyny, internalised homophobia, social permission for abuse, and exposure to violent sexual material.

### Safety note for application

This chapter should not be converted into a do-it-yourself interpretive exercise in situations involving fear, coercive control, physical violence, sexual violence, stalking, threats, or serious intimidation. In those situations, practical safety, risk assessment, and specialist professional support take priority over constructing a shared psychodynamic account. A couple formulation can otherwise obscure asymmetry and responsibility.

## 11.7 Love, hate, and the creative couple

Morgan's final position is that creativity does not arise from permanent harmony. The couple becomes more creative when it can acknowledge love and hate without either destroying the other.

Difference generates frustration, but it also generates thought. A relationship in which difference is abolished may feel safe but becomes psychically stagnant.

### Clinical example: loss of sexual desire after children

A couple in midlife reports a loving relationship but no sexual desire. Their disagreement about going away without the children opens access to long-buried hatred and feelings of abandonment from the child-rearing years. The wife has experienced the husband as selfish and demanding; the husband has experienced the wife as fused with the children and excluding him.

When hatred is named as real but containable, the couple can begin to think rather than simply withdraw or insist. Sexual deadness is connected to unrepresented relational history. The work then involves mourning the earlier version of the couple and creating a new one.

## 11.8 The book's final clinical ethic

The relationship remains alive through repeated cycles of:

1. encounter;
2. disruption;
3. recognition of difference;
4. contact with love and hate;
5. curiosity;
6. mourning of what no longer works;
7. reconfiguration;
8. creation of a new form of relating.

The couple's health is therefore not measured by the absence of conflict but by the capacity to keep conflict thinkable and transformable.

# 12. Lacanian crosswalk: how to use Morgan without conflating schools

**Important:** this section is an editorial bridge added for the Lacanian corpus. It is not Morgan's own theoretical claim. Terms are placed alongside one another because they can illuminate the same clinical material from different theories, not because they are synonyms.

## 12.1 Object relations versus Lacan's critique of object relation

Morgan's home tradition assumes that relationships to objects, internal and external, are central to psychic life. The partner can carry projected parts of self, reactivate internal objects, and become part of a developmental projective system.

Lacan's _Seminar IV: The Object Relation_ is an essential counter-text. At the start of that seminar Lacan explicitly questions whether analytic theory can be centred on a **dual relation between subject and object**. He reasserts the more complex relation of subject, ego, other, and the big Other. He also rejects the idea that analysis should culminate in adaptation to a naturally harmonious or adequately satisfying object.

### Use this contrast productively

When Morgan says:

> the partner receives or carries something projected by the subject

ask in Lacanian terms:

- Which signifier organizes the subject's position?
- What imaginary identification is operating?
- How is the partner recruited into the subject's fantasy?
- What demand is addressed to the Other?
- What satisfaction or jouissance is obtained from the repetitive scene?
- What role does the partner occupy in relation to object _a_?

Do not translate "projected part-object" directly into "object _a_". They belong to different metapsychologies.

## 12.2 Projective identification and the Imaginary

Morgan's projective identification is Kleinian/post-Kleinian. Lacan does not make it a central mechanism.

Some couple situations described by Morgan can nevertheless be re-read through Lacan's Imaginary:

- defining the partner in one's own image;
- rivalry and aggressivity;
- misrecognition;
- attempts to abolish difference;
- fascination with the similar other;
- dependence on the other's recognition;
- merger and claustrophobic capture.

The closest Lacanian analogue is often not "projection" but **imaginary capture plus a symbolic position in fantasy**.

### Key distinction

Morgan may ask: "What part of myself have I placed in my partner?"

A Lacanian enquiry may instead ask: "What position does this scene allow me to occupy, what signifier names me in it, and what does the repetition make possible for my desire or jouissance?"

Both questions can be useful. They are not the same question.

## 12.3 Unconscious partner choice and fantasy

Morgan explains partner choice through familiarity, internal objects, complementary projective systems, and repetition.

A Lacanian supplement would focus on **fantasy** as the frame through which the subject stages its relation to desire and to the object that causes desire.

Useful combined questions:

- What is consciously attractive about this partner?
- Which recurring trait in partners is disproportionally compelling?
- What scene do I repeatedly construct with lovers?
- What do I need to be for the other in that scene?
- What do I need the other to want from me?
- What intolerable possibility does the relationship help me avoid?
- What object-like function do I try to occupy: indispensable, abandoned, desired, rejected, rescued, rescuing, judged, betraying, betrayed, knowing, unknowable?

This cross-reading is particularly relevant to _Seminar VI: Desire and Its Interpretation_ and the Lacanian formula of fantasy, as well as Bruce Fink's exposition of the Lacanian subject.

## 12.4 Couple transference, repetition, automaton, and encounter

Morgan's couple transference emphasizes how past relationships are repeated between partners. Her link perspective adds that the partner also introduces novelty and interference.

This is a productive point of contact with _Seminar XI_. A Lacanian reading distinguishes the automaton of signifying repetition from the encounter with something that fails to fit the repetition.

Morgan's **interference** is not Lacan's _tuché_, but both concepts help prevent a theory in which the present is nothing but replayed childhood.

A useful question is:

> What repeats because the symbolic coordinates remain the same, and what in the partner repeatedly exceeds those coordinates?

## 12.5 Unconscious beliefs and master signifiers

Morgan's unconscious beliefs are propositions about relationships that function as unquestionable facts. Examples include "if you love me you should know", "true love means no ambivalence", or "disagreement means the bond is failing".

A Lacanian reader can often treat such statements as candidates for **master signifiers**, injunctions, or elements of the subject's discourse. The analytic task is not necessarily to replace a false belief with a correct belief but to examine how the signifier organizes the subject's position.

Useful procedure:

1. Write the recurrent sentence in the exact words in which it appears.
2. Identify the absolute terms: always, never, real, proper, loyal, selfish, safe, normal, enough.
3. Ask where this language comes from: family, culture, prior relationships, morality, fantasy.
4. Ask what happens when reality contradicts it.
5. Ask what identity would be endangered if the belief were loosened.
6. Ask what enjoyment is preserved by keeping the belief intact.

**Caution:** not every unconscious belief is literally an S1, and "S1" should not become a generic label for any strong opinion.

## 12.6 Narcissism, the small other, and alterity

Morgan's discussion of narcissism strongly overlaps with Lacanian problems of the Imaginary. The partner can become a mirror, rival, double, or extension of the ego. The demand for perfect attunement is a demand that the other cease to be other.

However, Morgan's **alterity** from link theory should not automatically be equated with Lacan's **big Other**.

- Morgan's alterity often refers to the irreducible difference of another concrete subject.
- Lacan's big Other is the locus of language, law, signification, and the symbolic order, not simply another person.
- Lacan's small other is closer to the specular counterpart in the Imaginary.

A clinically useful combined formulation is:

> The partner is simultaneously a concrete other person, an imaginary counterpart, a bearer of signifiers from the Other, and a figure recruited into fantasy. No one of these levels exhausts the relation.

## 12.7 "The relationship as a third" and Lacanian structure

Morgan's relational third is not the same as Lacan's symbolic third, paternal metaphor, big Other, or analytic discourse.

Yet the concept is valuable because it breaks the closed dyad. It encourages the couple to treat the recurrent relation itself as analysable.

A Lacanian reformulation might ask:

- What discourse are we repeatedly enacting?
- What position does each person occupy in relation to knowledge, demand, truth, and enjoyment?
- Which signifiers structure "us"?
- What does the relationship require each partner to sacrifice or perform?
- What product does the repetitive discourse generate?

_Seminar XVII_ can therefore be used as a more specifically Lacanian way to think beyond the two-person psychology, while Morgan provides richer concrete couple descriptions.

## 12.8 Couple state of mind and the analytic position

Morgan's couple state of mind is a reflective capacity: to move from "you versus me" toward seeing the pattern produced between us.

A Lacanian analyst would not necessarily make reflective mutual understanding the endpoint. Nonetheless, the move is clinically useful because it loosens imaginary dyadic capture.

The Lacanian addition is to ask what cannot be resolved simply by reaching a better shared representation. Some impasses are structural. Not everything between subjects can be made mutually transparent.

## 12.9 Desire: Morgan versus Lacan

Morgan uses desire in a broad clinical sense that includes sexual desire, erotic attraction, attachment, neurobiology, developmental history, and relationship dynamics.

Lacan's concept is more specific. Desire is distinguished from need and demand, is structured by language, and is bound to the desire of the Other. Drive and jouissance must also be kept distinct from desire.

Therefore:

- do not infer Lacanian desire from Morgan's references to libido or sexual motivation;
- use Morgan to identify the **scene and relational context** of desire;
- use Lacan to analyze the **structure of desire, demand, fantasy, object cause, and jouissance**.

## 12.10 Sexual desire and the non-existence of a pre-given sexual relation

Morgan repeatedly emphasizes that two bodies and two psyches do not naturally fit, that desire changes, and that difference must be negotiated. She treats this primarily as a developmental and relational fact.

_Seminar XX: Encore_ radicalizes the problem. Lacan's formula that there is no pre-written sexual relation means there is no symbolic formula guaranteeing complementary fit between sexed subjects.

Morgan's concepts of uncertainty, interference, and the irreducible other are therefore valuable **clinical companions** to _Encore_, but not explanations of Lacan's formula.

A useful combined question is:

> What fantasy of natural complementarity does this couple demand from sex, and what happens when the actual sexual relation fails to instantiate it?

## 12.11 Love

Morgan's developmental distinction between romantic and mature love should be kept separate from Lacan's various formulations of love.

For Morgan, mature love tends toward integration, recognition of separateness, ambivalence, concern, gratitude, forgiveness, and curiosity.

Lacan treats love in several changing ways across his teaching, including love in transference, love as metaphor, love and lack, love and the object, and later love in relation to the impossibility of sexual complementarity.

_Seminar VIII: Transference_ and Bruce Fink's work on Lacan and love are the most useful cross-reads.

**Critical caution:** do not make Morgan's mature/depressive-position love into a Lacanian normative end point. Lacan does not simply replace narcissistic love with psychologically mature reciprocal object love as the telos of analysis.

## 12.12 Anxiety

Morgan describes anxieties of engulfment, abandonment, exclusion, vulnerability, and difference. These are clinically rich descriptions.

_Seminar X: Anxiety_ provides a different metapsychology, centred on anxiety's relation to desire, the Other, lack, and object _a_. Morgan's language can identify the interpersonal scene; Lacan can help analyze why the scene becomes anxiety-producing.

Avoid simple equivalences such as "engulfment anxiety = object a". The useful procedure is to ask what happens to lack, desire, and the subject's position when the partner becomes too present, too absent, too knowing, or too demanding.

## 12.13 Curiosity, knowledge, and the subject supposed to know

Morgan values curiosity about the partner and contrasts it with certainty. This is one of the best bridges to a Lacanian clinical ethic, provided it is not reduced to information-gathering.

Lacan's work on transference and the subject supposed to know reminds us that the wish to know the other can itself become a fantasy. The aim is not complete transparency between partners.

Useful distinction:

- **curiosity:** willingness to let the other surprise one's knowledge;
- **intrusiveness:** attempt to abolish the other's opacity;
- **analysis:** attention to the fact that the wish to know also has an unconscious structure.

## 12.14 Violence and Lacanian interpretation

Morgan's violence chapter is a necessary practical corrective to over-intellectualisation. Coercive control, physical violence, sexual violence, threats, and severe intimidation cannot safely be reduced to mutual fantasy or a symmetrical couple system.

A Lacanian formulation may later illuminate structure, jouissance, identification, or discourse, but immediate safety and asymmetrical responsibility must not be erased in the name of a sophisticated couple interpretation.

# 13. Concept glossary for retrieval in Gemini Notebook

## Alterity

The irreducible otherness of another subject. In Morgan's link-theory usage, alterity is what resists assimilation to the self's categories.

**Do not confuse with:** Lacan's big Other.

## Couple fit

The conscious and unconscious compatibility that allows two partners' needs, conflicts, projections, and capacities to interlock.

## Couple projective system

A recurrent division and circulation of projected psychic states between partners. It can be developmental or defensive.

## Couple state of mind

The ability to experience the relationship from within while also observing what the couple is creating together.

## Creative couple

A couple function in which difference can be tolerated and brought into contact without a predetermined outcome, permitting new thought or action to emerge.

## Interference

The disturbance created by the real presence of an other subject who cannot be fully assimilated to transference expectations or prior categories.

## Interpsychic

The dimension of psychic life arising through the mutual influence of two psyches.

## Joint ego ideal

The couple's developing shared image of what their relationship should aspire to be.

## Joint superego

The shared prohibitions, moral standards, and evaluative structure generated within the couple. It can support concern and commitment or become punitive.

## Link

A psychic reality created between subjects that both emerges from them and subsequently shapes them.

## Narcissistic pact

An unconscious arrangement that protects the partners from the anxiety of separateness and difference by organising fusion, domination/submission, or restricted sharing of psychic space.

## Presence

The capacity of another person's actual existence to affect and modify the subject.

## Proleptic imagination

A state in which an anticipated story becomes emotionally equivalent to reality before reality has been established.

## Projective gridlock

A couple arrangement based on mutual projective identification that creates the fantasy of living inside one another and reduces separateness.

## Shared defence

A couple-level defensive arrangement that protects both partners against a shared anxiety.

## Shared unconscious fantasy

A jointly reinforced fantasy or emotional expectation that structures how the couple interprets and responds to events.

## Unconscious alliance

A relational agreement that helps realise wishes and keep threatening psychic material excluded from awareness.

## Unconscious belief

A fantasy or proposition felt as self-evident reality and defended against modification by new experience.

# 15. A structured self-analysis protocol based on Morgan, with Lacanian extensions

This framework is designed for reflection, not self-diagnosis. It is most useful when completed **after** emotional intensity has reduced.

## Step 1: Describe the event without interpretation

Write what happened in observable sequence.

- What was said?
- What was done?
- What was the immediate context?
- What third factor was present: child, work, money, family, phone, sex, illness, time, another person?

Avoid motives at this stage.

## Step 2: Record the first internal meaning

Complete the sentence:

> When this happened, it meant to me that...

Examples: I do not matter; I am trapped; I am being abandoned; I am being controlled; I am undesirable; I am being humiliated; I cannot trust; I am alone; I am not allowed a separate mind.

## Step 3: Identify the affect that was hardest to hold

Name the feeling beneath the argument if possible:

- fear;
- shame;
- envy;
- jealousy;
- helplessness;
- dependency;
- humiliation;
- rage;
- grief;
- sexual frustration;
- guilt;
- exclusion;
- inadequacy.

Then ask:

> Did I experience this as my feeling, or did I become certain it belonged to my partner?

## Step 4: Identify the role assigned to the partner

Complete:

> In my version of the scene, my partner became the one who...

Examples: abandons, judges, withholds, controls, needs too much, refuses sex, knows better, cannot cope, betrays, depends, rescues, ignores.

Then ask:

> Is this a description, a recurrent fantasy, or both?

## Step 5: Identify your own complementary position

Complete:

> This allowed or forced me to become the one who...

Examples: rescues, submits, attacks, pursues, withdraws, proves, teaches, pleads, refuses, sacrifices, knows, is victimised, controls.

This is where the couple system becomes visible.

## Step 6: Search for the unconscious belief

Write the relationship law implied by the conflict.

Examples:

- If you loved me, you would know without being told.
- Partners should always choose each other over everyone else.
- Needing space means rejection.
- Good sex should be spontaneous.
- Anger means love is gone.
- If we disagree about parenting, one of us is a bad parent.
- Privacy is secrecy.
- If I am vulnerable, I will lose power.

Ask:

> What would become frightening if this law were not true?

## Step 7: Examine the evidence you excluded

Ask:

- What did my partner actually say that does not fit my story?
- What fact did I minimize?
- What alternative interpretation did I refuse because it produced uncertainty?
- Did I treat my emotional truth as proof of external fact?

This is the Morgan/Stokoe move from certainty toward curiosity.

## Step 8: Identify the couple's shared defence

Ask what the recurring pattern prevents both partners from encountering.

Examples:

- constant arguing prevents vulnerable dependence;
- constant closeness prevents separateness;
- sexual avoidance prevents exposure to desire and rejection;
- parenting conflict prevents grief about loss of the pre-children couple;
- financial arguments prevent discussion of power or dependency;
- intellectual analysis prevents affective encounter.

## Step 9: Identify interference

Ask:

> What did my partner do or say that did not fit my established version of them?

This is often the most important line. It identifies where the real other is resisting the script.

## Step 10: Take the third position

Replace:

> What are you doing to me?

with:

> What are we producing between us when this begins?

Then assign responsibility separately:

- What is mine?
- What is theirs?
- What belongs to the recurrent pattern?

The third position does not erase individual responsibility.

## Step 11: Lacanian extension: isolate the signifiers

Write down the exact charged words repeated in the conflict.

Examples: selfish, enough, proper, loyal, safe, controlling, needy, cold, childish, normal, respect, trust, freedom.

Ask:

- Who first used these words in my history?
- What identity do they confer on me?
- Which signifier do I most fear being named by?
- What word do I repeatedly use to fix my partner in place?

## Step 12: Lacanian extension: locate desire and demand

Distinguish:

- What am I asking for explicitly?
- What demand for love or recognition is embedded in the request?
- What do I want that cannot be satisfied by compliance alone?
- If my partner gave me exactly what I ask for, what dissatisfaction might remain?

## Step 13: Lacanian extension: locate the repetitive satisfaction

Ask a difficult question:

> What do I get from this scene even when I hate it?

Possible answers include certainty, innocence, moral superiority, confirmation of being abandoned, proof of being indispensable, permission to withdraw, intensity, sexualized conflict, rescue, punishment, or avoidance of another desire.

The point is not to blame oneself. It is to identify why insight alone may not stop repetition.

## Step 14: Define one experiment in curiosity

Choose a small action that does not require agreement on the full interpretation.

Examples:

- ask one question before explaining the partner's motive;
- state the feeling without assigning causality;
- tolerate ten minutes of disagreement without forcing resolution;
- name the couple pattern when it begins;
- distinguish "I feel abandoned" from "you are abandoning me";
- allow a request for space without treating it immediately as rejection;
- ask what sex, silence, money, or parenting means to the other rather than assuming.

# 16. Couple-therapy use: a session formulation template

A therapist or a couple using the book for structured reflection can organize material under the following headings.

## A. Presenting conflict

What does the couple say the problem is?

## B. Intrapsychic histories

What early experiences, internal objects, attachment expectations, family myths, trauma, sexuality, and prior relationships appear relevant for each partner?

## C. Unconscious partner fit

What capacities, conflicts, or disowned parts seem to fit together?

## D. Projective circulation

Which affects or roles move between partners? Who usually carries anger, helplessness, competence, sexuality, dependency, shame, or certainty?

## E. Shared unconscious fantasy or belief

What story about relationships seems to operate as fact?

## F. Shared defence or alliance

What does the couple do together to avoid the anxiety generated by that fantasy?

## G. Narcissism and alterity

Where does one partner have difficulty allowing the other a separate mind, desire, or experience?

## H. Interference

Where is the actual other resisting the old pattern?

## I. The couple third

What has the relationship itself come to require or prohibit?

## J. Capacity for third position

Can the couple observe the pattern together, or does every interpretation collapse back into blame?

## K. Love, hate, and curiosity

Which affects can be thought and spoken? Which must be denied, projected, or enacted?

## L. Sexuality

What explicit and unconscious meanings do sex, desire, fidelity, exclusivity, openness, pornography, body changes, or lack of sex carry?

## M. Lacanian supplement

What signifiers, demands, fantasies, identifications, jouissance, and positions in discourse organise the same material?

## N. Safety and asymmetry

Is there fear, coercive control, violence, sexual coercion, stalking, threats, or a major power asymmetry that makes a symmetrical couple formulation misleading or unsafe?

# 17. Common interpretive errors when using this book for self-analysis

## 17.1 Turning a concept into a diagnosis of the partner

Bad use:

> You are projecting your anger into me.

Better use:

> I notice I become the angry one in our conflicts and you become the reasonable one. I want to understand how we both contribute to that pattern.

The concepts are most useful when they increase uncertainty productively, not when they become weapons of certainty.

## 17.2 Treating every conflict as mutual and symmetrical

Some patterns are co-created. Some actions are not symmetrical. Deception, coercion, abuse, threats, and violence require specific accountability.

## 17.3 Assuming insight automatically changes the pattern

A couple can understand its dynamic very well and continue repeating it. The pattern may provide satisfaction, stability, identity, or protection against a more frightening problem.

## 17.4 Treating "otherness" as moral virtue

Difference is not automatically good. The value lies in being able to perceive and think about difference. Some differences are incompatible with the continuation of a relationship.

## 17.5 Confusing curiosity with surveillance

Curiosity accepts the other's right to opacity. Intrusiveness seeks possession of the other's interior.

## 17.6 Making "mature love" into an idealized standard

Morgan's mature love model is useful, but it can itself become an unconscious couple belief. No relationship maintains perfect concern, curiosity, gratitude, and reflective function.

## 17.7 Conflating object relations with Lacan

Terms such as projective identification, internal object, depressive position, containment, mentalization, and couple projective system are not Lacanian terms. Use them as an additional clinical vocabulary.

# 18. High-value Lacanian cross-reading sequence

The following sequence makes Morgan most useful inside the existing Lacanian corpus.

## 18.1 Object relation and the critique of the dyad

**Read with:** Jacques Lacan, _Seminar IV: The Object Relation_ (1956-1957).

**Question:** what is missed when the couple is explained primarily as a relation between two objects or two egos?

## 18.2 Imaginary capture, aggressivity, and narcissism

**Read with:** Lacan, "The Mirror Stage" and "Aggressiveness in Psychoanalysis" in _Écrits_.

**Question:** how do merger, rivalry, misrecognition, and the demand for similarity structure couple conflict?

## 18.3 Desire and fantasy

**Read with:** _Seminar V: Formations of the Unconscious_; _Seminar VI: Desire and Its Interpretation_; Fink, _The Lacanian Subject_.

**Question:** what does the partner's place in the subject's fantasy explain that projective identification alone does not?

## 18.4 Love and transference

**Read with:** _Seminar VIII: Transference_; Bruce Fink, "Freud and Lacan on Love: A Preliminary Exploration"; ideally the full _Lacan on Love_ if acquired.

**Question:** how does love relate to lack, desire, agalma, transference, and the wish to be what the other lacks?

## 18.5 Anxiety and the other's desire

**Read with:** _Seminar X: Anxiety_.

**Question:** what becomes anxiety-producing when the other is too close, too absent, too desiring, too knowing, or impossible to locate?

## 18.6 Repetition and the encounter

**Read with:** _Seminar XI: The Four Fundamental Concepts of Psychoanalysis_.

**Question:** what in the couple is signifying repetition, and what remains an encounter that escapes the established script?

## 18.7 Discourse and the couple as structure

**Read with:** _Seminar XVII: The Other Side of Psychoanalysis_.

**Question:** can a recurrent couple scene be read as a circulation of positions rather than as two personalities colliding?

## 18.8 Sexual non-relation and love

**Read with:** _Seminar XX: Encore_; Alenka Zupančič, _What Is Sex?_; Colette Soler, _What Lacan Said About Women_.

**Question:** what demand for complementarity is being made of sex or love, and how does the relationship respond to the impossibility of complete fit?

# 19. Highest-value concepts to import into the Lacanian notebook

If only a small part of this file is retained, preserve the following concepts.

1. **Couple state of mind**: the capacity to observe the relation one is inside.
2. **Interference**: the partner's real otherness disrupts the repetition of the past.
3. **Uncertainty**: the couple link creates something not fully predetermined.
4. **Unconscious belief**: a relationship proposition felt as unquestionable fact.
5. **Proleptic imagination**: an anticipated relational story becomes emotionally real before evidence is established.
6. **Shared defence**: the couple jointly organizes a way not to encounter a shared anxiety.
7. **Narcissistic pact**: the relationship is structured to eliminate the threat of separateness.
8. **Curiosity versus intrusiveness**: genuine interest requires accepting that the other cannot be fully possessed or known.
9. **Creative couple**: difference becomes generative rather than merely conflictual.
10. **Love and hate must both be thinkable**: denial of aggression can deaden love and sexuality.

# 22. Final synthesis

Morgan's book is worth adding to the corpus because it fills a gap that primary Lacanian texts do not try to fill in the same way: it gives a compact, contemporary, clinically grounded map of **how two people unconsciously construct a couple relationship**.

Its most valuable contribution is not a theory of individual psychopathology. It is a vocabulary for the relation itself:

- what the two bring from the past;
- what they make each other carry;
- what beliefs they jointly install;
- what defences they build together;
- how they use certainty to suppress the other's difference;
- how the actual presence of the other disrupts repetition;
- how a relationship becomes a third entity;
- how desire can be affected by merger, hostility, shame, or parenthood;
- how love and hate coexist;
- how a couple can regain curiosity and create a new form rather than simply restore the old one.

For a Lacanian project, the strongest use is **triangulation**. Morgan provides the clinical phenomenology of couple systems. Lacan provides a different structural account of subject, signifier, fantasy, desire, object _a_, jouissance, the Other, and sexual non-relation. Reading one through the other without collapsing them gives a substantially richer instrument for self-analysis and for thinking about couple therapy.

The single most useful discipline to retain is this:

> When certainty about the partner becomes strongest, increase attention to the structure of the scene and decrease confidence that the partner has already been fully understood.

That principle is consistent with Morgan's emphasis on curiosity and also creates the right opening for Lacanian analysis: not the fantasy of complete mutual understanding, but a more precise encounter with what each subject says, repeats, demands, desires, and cannot fully know about themselves or the other.
